Traditional Tea Houses in Fuzhou

Fuzhou is a city where tea is drunk through the day rather than in a single sitting. The tea counters in Fuzhou sit on the commercial streets, so a tea stop and a sightseeing stop are usually the same stop.

Tea houses around the old quarter

Most of Fuzhou's tea trade happens in the streets around the old quarter, where the tea shops pour a pot for you at the counter rather than selling by the tin. Ask for a longjing, a shou pu-erh or a jasmine blend and they will let you taste before you buy.

Morning tea and the market stalls

Tea drinking in Fuzhou starts early: the markets open with the first kettle of the day, and the stalls around the old commercial streets are where the city's own blend is put together.

Overview: Tea Culture in Fuzhou

Fuzhou is the home of scented jasmine tea: green tea layered with freshly picked jasmine over several nights, a technique the city has run at commercial scale since the 19th century. The grade ladder climbs from three scenting rounds to nine for the finest lots, and the craft is woven through the old lane quarter of Sanfang Qixiang, where covered bowls of it are poured all day.

The tea

Jasmine tea (茉莉花茶)

Jasmine tea is scented rather than fermented: green tea is layered with fresh jasmine blossoms over several nights in Fuzhou, so the scent is absorbed rather than added. The flowers are picked in the evening, when they are at their strongest. Standard grades take three scenting rounds, top lots up to nine, and the spent blossoms are then removed - the best packs show leaf only.

How to have it here: In Fuzhou it is an everyday pot rather than a special occasion: covered bowls in the lanes of Sanfang Qixiang, corner shops and breakfast tables. Labels state the scenting count, from three rounds upward. The base tea is spring-harvested, then scented in July and August when the jasmine crop peaks, so a finished lot carries two dates in effect.

Lapsang Souchong (正山小种)

Lapsang Souchong comes from Tongmu Village in the Wuyi mountains of northern Fujian, about 300 km from Fuzhou. The leaves are withered over pine fires, which gives the black tea its smoke character - the original 'black tea' of the China trade, recorded at Tongmu from the 17th century. It is sold across Fuzhou's tea shops alongside the local jasmine.

How to have it here: Brew it at 90-95°C for two to three minutes; the smoke stands up to milk in the Hong Kong manner. Most drinkers in Fuzhou treat it as the regional black-tea option beside jasmine, and the producing village of Tongmu is reachable by a roughly 300 km trip north to Wuyishan if you want the workshops themselves.

Jasmine tea etiquette in Fuzhou

The vessel is the gaiwan, a covered bowl of about 150 ml holding roughly 5 grams of leaf. The lid does double duty: held slightly ajar to pour, then lifted to the nose and inhaled before the first sip, because jasmine grades are judged on the scent first. Water around 85-90°C, first steep about 45 seconds, three refills as a rule.

Fuzhou pours for the table, not for the individual: the host fills guests' cups first, in age order, and keeps the pot moving. A guest taps two fingers when topped up. Filling a cup to the brim is poor form - seven- or eight-tenths full is the local standard - and asking how many scenting rounds a lot took, three or nine, is the ordinary way to talk quality.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does the scenting count on the label mean?

It is the number of times the base green tea was layered with fresh jasmine blossoms - from three rounds in standard grades up to nine in the finest. More rounds mean more flowers and more labour, so the price scales with the count. Top lots end with the spent blossoms removed entirely, leaving leaf only in the pack.

Why is Fuzhou associated with jasmine tea?

The city has run commercial scenting since the 19th century, sitting between jasmine-growing fields on the plain and green-tea producers in the hills. The July-August blossom season, the several-night layering method and the evening picking of the flowers all point back to Fuzhou workshops, and Sanfang Qixiang teahouses still pour it as the local default.

Is Lapsang Souchong also from Fuzhou?

No. It is a black tea from Tongmu Village in the Wuyi mountains of northern Fujian, about 300 km from Fuzhou, withered over pine fires since the 17th century. It is stocked in Fuzhou as part of the wider Fujian tea trade, but its origin is the mountain country, not the capital of the province's scenting craft.

Where can I drink it in Fuzhou?

Teahouses in the Sanfang Qixiang lane quarter serve it in covered bowls, the standard 150 ml vessel with about 5 grams of leaf. Tea shops across the city grade by the scenting count printed on the pack, and tasting two or three counts side by side before buying is a normal request, not a special favour.
