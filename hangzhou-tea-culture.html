Overview: Tea Culture in Hangzhou

Hangzhou has grown tea for more than twelve centuries. The Tang tea scholar Lu Yu, writing the Classic of Tea around 760-780 CE, recorded that the Qianlong prefecture produced tea at two temples, Tianzhu and Lingyin. The name that dominates the city today is West Lake Longjing, a pan-fired green tea from the villages west of the lake, and in August 2021 the authorities confirmed the start of building a national geographical-indication protection demonstration zone for it.

The tea

West Lake Longjing (西湖龙井)

Longjing is a pan-fired green tea named after the Longjing village in the hills behind West Lake in Hangzhou. The buds and first leaves are picked before Qingming, fired by hand, and flattened into flat, leaf-shaped cakes. The finished leaf is a bud with one or two leaves, narrow and evenly aligned, green-yellow in colour, and the standard describes its four excellences as green colour, rich fragrance, sweet taste and fine sparrow-tongue shape. The spring cut is taken before Qingming, which falls around April 4-5, and the pick in early April is the one the market waits for each year.

How to have it here: The villages west of the lake supply the protected tea: Shifeng and Longjing village for the Shi and Long marks, and Meijiawu, Fancun, Yunqi and Wai Daqiao for the Mei mark, with the Long mark covering the rest of West Lake township. Grading follows the calendar, with Mingqian picked before Qingming around April 4-5 at the top, then Yuqian before Grain Rain around April 20. At the source villages the tea is poured in tall glasses, and the classic local pairing is Hupao Spring water, about 5 km south of the lake.

Longjing of the Qiantang and Yuezhou areas (龙井茶)

Longjing is a pan-fired green tea named after the Longjing village in the hills behind West Lake in Hangzhou. Chinese national standards allow only tea from three production areas to carry the Longjing series name: West Lake, Qiantang in Hangzhou prefecture and Yuezhou in Shaoxing. The protected area covers 12 counties, cities and districts including Xihu, Yuhang, Lin'an, Fuyang, Xiaoshan, Tonglu, Chun'an, Jiande, Shaoxing, Zhuji, Shengzhou and Xinchang, plus parts of four further counties on the Shaoxing border.

How to have it here: The Longjing designation is now a legal mark rather than a loose description, so a shop in another province selling tea as Longjing without naming a protected origin area is selling outside the standard. Longjing is also grown outside the mainland: the Sanxia district of New Taipei City, planted during the Japanese colonial period and expanded afterwards for officials who preferred Longjing and jasmine, was its only Taiwanese producing area as of 2010, and a local growers' association has held a best-lot competition there since 2010.

Teahouses

China National Tea Museum (中国茶叶博物馆)

A dedicated museum of tea and tea culture in the West Lake District of Hangzhou, with two sites, one at Shuangfeng Village and one at Longjing Village in the hills behind the lake. It opened to the public in April 1991 and is listed by the China Museums Association as a national first-class museum, so its two compounds function as a working reference for the whole sequence of processing and for the classification of tea in Chinese history.

Area: West Lake District, Hangzhou, at Shuangfeng Village and Longjing Village

Qingteng Teahouse (青藤茶馆)

A teahouse on Nanshan Road that appears in published lists of the city's intangible-heritage tea spaces, and is written about in the local trade press for the way it positions itself around the arrival of the new spring tea, using tasting tables and seasonal programming rather than a single fixed menu. It has been trading under the same name since 1996. Hours are not published in the sources consulted.

Area: Nanshan Road, Hangzhou

How Hangzhou drinks Longjing

Longjing is brewed plain: about 3 grams of leaf in a 150-200 ml glass, water cooled to 80-85 degrees, one to two minutes for the first infusion. The glass rather than a lidded bowl is deliberate, since the flattened leaf stands on end and settles slowly and the glass shows it. Two or three infusions are normal, and hot-but-not-boiling water keeps the sweetness in the leaf instead of driving it out.

Refill etiquette is practical rather than ceremonial: drink down to about a third of the glass before the host tops it up, and a good host never lets a guest's glass run dry. Tapping two bent fingers on the table acknowledges a pour, a gesture used across Jiangnan tea tables. Cups stay small, because Longjing is meant to be refilled slowly rather than drained in one go, and the same 150-200 ml glass and 80-85 degree water are used from the first round to the last.

In the village tasting rooms, the sequence of questions matters more than the price. Ask what the picking date was, whether the leaf is Mingqian or Yuqian, and which of the Shi, Long or Mei marks the tea carries, out of the 5 village names once in use and 3 kept now, then watch the fix before you buy, since the pan-firing is done by hand. Shops that sell only by the hill without naming a village or a date are not describing a grade you can verify.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which grade of Longjing should I buy?

The top grade is Mingqian, picked before Qingming around April 4-5; the next cut is Yuqian, before Grain Rain around April 20. Prices fall sharply after Qingming as the leaf grows larger, so mid-to-late April is the practical window for good value at the village shops. Ask for the picking date in writing if the price is high.

What do the Shi, Long and Mei marks on a tin mean?

They are village marks. In the early 20th century the tea sold under five designations, Shi, Long, Yun, Hu and Mei, later reduced to three: Shi for Shifeng and Longjing village, Mei for Meijiawu, Fancun, Yunqi and Wai Daqiao, and Long for the remainder of West Lake township. A tin carrying a village mark is describing an origin, not a quality tier.

Where can I taste Longjing at the source?

Longjing village, Shifeng and Meijiawu sit in the hills west of West Lake and are reachable by bus in under an hour from downtown. Farmhouses pour tastings through the spring picking, and the China National Tea Museum keeps sites at Shuangfeng Village and Longjing Village, opened in April 1991. Hupao Spring, whose water has been paired with the tea for centuries, is a separate park about 5 km south of the lake.

How do I brew Longjing at home?

Use 3 grams of leaf per 150 ml glass, water at 80-85 degrees, and steep one to two minutes for the first infusion, then add 10 seconds per round. Boiling water scorches the bud and flattens the aroma. Longjing expands well in a glass, so a second and third infusion stay light rather than turning bitter, and a 200 ml glass is the size most often used in the village houses.

What did the 2021 protection zone change for buyers?

In August 2021 the authorities confirmed the start of building a national geographical-indication product protection demonstration zone for West Lake Longjing, which puts the protection area on an administrative footing rather than a customary one. Separately, national standards already restrict the Longjing name to the West Lake, Qiantang and Yuezhou production areas, so a label without an origin area is not describing protected tea.

Is Longjing grown outside mainland China?

Yes. Sanxia in New Taipei City grew red tea during the Japanese colonial period and switched after 1945 to the styles that senior Kuomintang officials preferred, Longjing and jasmine among them. As of 2010 it was Taiwan's only Longjing producing area, and the Sanxia farmers' association has held an annual best-tea competition there since that year, judged on the same picking standards as the mainland villages.
