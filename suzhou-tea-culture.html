Overview: Tea Culture in Suzhou

Biluochun grows on two peninsula hills in Lake Tai - the Dongting East and West hills, roughly 35 km southwest of Suzhou's old city. First known as 'scary fragrance', the tea was renamed by the Kangxi Emperor around 1700. Its picking standard is extreme: one bud with one unfolded leaf, adding up to about 60,000 picks per 500 grams of finished tea. The city was also the finest jasmine tea district in China from the Southern Song onward, and that trade is now nearly gone from the market.

The tea

Biluochun (碧螺春)

Biluochun is a tightly rolled green tea from Dongting Mountain on the shore of Lake Tai near Suzhou. The whole leaf - bud and leaf together - is rolled into small spirals and scented once with jasmine blossoms, which is where the name 'snail shell spring tea' comes from. The strands are fine, tightly coiled and densely covered with white down, silver-green over the folded leaf. Picking runs from the spring equinox to Grain Rain, with sorting done at midday and the pan work carried out from the afternoon into the evening. About 60,000 picks stand behind 500 grams of finished tea, since the standard is a single bud with one unfolded leaf.

How to have it here: Top grades are brewed in glass so the spirals can be seen sinking slowly, and the finest can even be opened with cold mineral or hill spring water, a test that weaker leaf cannot pass. The tea is designated for the Great Hall of the People, where it is served to visiting foreign guests and used as a presentation gift. The hillside picking is done by hand in the morning, with the shoots sorted to remove fishy leaves, old leaves and long stems before the wok. The pan sequence runs to 3 stages - fixation, rolling with pressing, and rubbing into balls while drying.

Huqiu jasmine tea (虎丘茉莉花茶)

Jasmine tea is scented rather than fermented: green tea is layered with fresh jasmine blossoms over several nights, so the scent is absorbed rather than added. The flowers are picked in the evening, when they are at their strongest. Suzhou was the finest jasmine tea district in the whole of China from the Southern Song dynasty, which began in 1127, and its method was exacting, because the quality demanded of the blossoms was correspondingly high and the making involved several stages of care.

How to have it here: Huqiu jasmine tea stands in a Suzhou speciality list beside Yangcheng Lake hairy crabs, Dongting Biluochun, Huangdai watermelon seeds and Dongshan citrus, bayberry and loquat, which is how the tea is normally presented in the city. The Suzhou trade has contracted badly against the coarse, low-cost processing model used in Guangxi's Hengxian, and jasmine tea grown in Suzhou itself is now hard to find in the market, although the tea still stands on the list of city specialities alongside the crabs and the citrus of a city with more than 4,000 years of recorded history.

How Suzhou serves Biluochun

Biluochun is brewed light. About 3 grams to a 150 ml glass, water at 80-85 degrees, and a first steep of 45 to 60 seconds, because the rolled spirals hold a great deal of surface area and release fast. A good cup should show a clear green liquor and a settled bed of whole leaves rather than dust, so a glass rather than a porcelain cup is the usual choice, and a pot is generally left alone. Two or three rounds are enough; a fourth is thin.

The serving order follows the district, not the price. Tea from the Dongting hills comes in grades that run from the first spring pick through later cuts to the coarser summer leaf, and the picking date from the spring equinox, around 20 March, to Grain Rain, around 20 April, matters more to a Suzhou buyer than any label on the tin. Pouring for others before yourself is the local rule, and refilling a guest's glass before your own is the older habit that survives at home rather than in restaurants.

The same morning rhythm runs on noodles as well as tea: Suzhou-style noodle soup is one of the city's own morning-tea foods, and the shops that serve it open early, so the tea table and the noodle shop belong to a single breakfast habit, on a street cut in 825 to link the walled city with the hill of Tiger Hill.

Because the local jasmine trade has contracted to the point where Suzhou-grown scented tea is hard to buy, treat any claim of 'Suzhou jasmine' in a shop as worth checking against the grade rather than accepting it as standard. The same caution applies to the famous name itself: the tea has been designated for the Great Hall of the People, so counterfeits of the premium grade are common, and the certificates that accompany the tin carry the district and the picking date, with 1 of the 2 Dongting hills named on each.

Frequently Asked Questions

How did a tea called scary fragrance become Biluochun?

The account recorded in Wang Yingkui's Liunan Suibian runs that wild tea trees grew on the cliff face of Biluofeng on Dongting Mountain, and that in a good harvest year a girl filling her basket put the leaves inside her bodice against her skin; the body heat released an overwhelming fragrance and she cried out 'scary fragrance'. In the 38th year of Kangxi, 1699, the Wu county magistrate Song Luo bought the tea and presented it at the imperial southern tour, and the emperor renamed it Biluochun.

How is Biluochun actually made?

The picking runs from the spring equinox, around 20 March, to Grain Rain, around 20 April, and takes 1 bud with 1 leaf. Sorting follows at midday to remove fishy leaves, old leaves and over-long stems, and the pan work runs from the afternoon into the evening. Most production is still by hand, in 3 stages performed in the same wok: fixation, rolling with pressing, and rubbing into balls while drying. The rubbing and drying step, in which the downy hairs are raised, is the decisive part of the craft.

Why is Biluochun shaped and coloured the way it is?

The local growing method explains the finished leaf: tea trees are planted in rows interleaved with plum and loquat, because tea absorbs any nearby scent, and the blossom scent of those two fruit trees enters the leaf over generations of selection. The red soil of the hills is free-draining even when pressed into a ball, and the moisture rising off Lake Tai, a lake of 2,338 square kilometres with 48 islands and 72 peaks, completes the local microclimate, which is why the tea carries both a floral and a fruit note distinct from other green teas.

What made Suzhou famous for jasmine tea?

Suzhou was the best jasmine tea district in China from the Southern Song dynasty, which began in 1127, and the method there was complex and exacting, with correspondingly strict requirements on the quality of the blossoms used for scenting. That raised the cost, and after the coarse, low-cost processing model of Hengxian in Guangxi spread through the market, tea actually grown in Suzhou became almost impossible to find, even though the Huqiu jasmine name survives in the city's list of specialities, where 2 of the 6 listed products are teas.

Where exactly are the two Dongting hills?

They stand in Lake Tai, the third largest of China's five great freshwater lakes, with an area of 2,338 square kilometres, a surface altitude of 3.33 metres, a maximum depth of 4.8 metres, a shoreline of about 400 km, 48 islands and 72 peaks. Xishan, the western Dongting hill, is the largest island in the lake: 11 km from north to south, 15 km from east to west, and 79.8 square kilometres in area.

Can Biluochun be brewed with cold water, and where do people drink it in Suzhou?

The top grade can be opened with cold mineral water or cold hill spring water, and the leaf will still sink slowly and release its fragrance, a test no other tea passes; ordinary grades will not behave this way, and in the villages the usual brew is a hot infusion in a 150 ml glass. The drinking is in the old quarters: Shantang Street was cut in 825 by Bai Juyi, Pingjiang Road was recorded in 1834 with 10 wells, and Guanqian Street's south side still carries a lane over 200 metres long named for Ming eunuchs.
