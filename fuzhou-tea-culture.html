Overview: Tea Culture in Fuzhou

Fuzhou is the home of scented jasmine tea: green tea layered with freshly picked jasmine over several nights, a technique the city has run at commercial scale since the 19th century. The grade ladder climbs from 3 scenting rounds to 9 for the finest lots, and the craft is woven through the old lane quarter of Sanfang Qixiang, where covered bowls of it are poured all day. The city was named jasmine as its municipal flower in 1985, registered Fuzhou jasmine tea as a geographical indication in 2008, and saw the Fuzhou branch of the traditional tea craft inscribed on the UNESCO intangible heritage list in 2022.

The tea

Jasmine tea (茉莉花茶)

Jasmine tea is scented rather than fermented: green tea is layered with fresh jasmine blossoms over several nights in Fuzhou, so the scent is absorbed rather than added. The flowers are picked in the evening, when they are at their strongest. Standard grades take 3 scenting rounds, top lots up to 9, and the spent blossoms are then removed - the best packs show leaf only.

How to have it here: In Fuzhou it is an everyday pot rather than a special occasion: covered bowls in the lanes of Sanfang Qixiang, corner shops and breakfast tables. Labels state the scenting count, from 3 rounds upward. The base tea is spring-harvested, then scented in July and August when the jasmine crop peaks, so a finished lot carries two dates in effect - a picking date for the leaf and a month for the flower. Fuzhou growers have cultivated jasmine for several hundred years and lead the country in both output and grade, which is why the city counts as the birthplace of the style.

Lapsang Souchong (正山小种)

Lapsang Souchong comes from Tongmu Village in the Wuyi mountains of northern Fujian, about 300 km from Fuzhou. The leaves are withered over pine fires, which gives the black tea its smoke character - the original 'black tea' of the China trade, recorded at Tongmu from the 17th century. It is sold across Fuzhou's tea shops alongside the local jasmine.

How to have it here: Brew it at 90-95°C for two to three minutes; the smoke stands up to milk in the Hong Kong manner. Most drinkers in Fuzhou treat it as the regional black-tea option beside jasmine, and the producing village of Tongmu is reachable by a roughly 300 km trip north to Wuyishan if you want the workshops themselves. The name is attested before 1640, and Dutch traders were already shipping small-leaf black tea from the region to Europe by 1610, so the style is older than the trade terms that still carry it.

Tieguanyin (铁观音)

Tieguanyin is a semi-fermented oolong from Anxi County in southern Fujian. The leaves are rolled into dark, tightly packed pellets, and the same pot can be brewed 8 or 10 times, with each round sweeter than the last. Anxi sits inland in the province's southern tea country, well away from Fuzhou, but the tea is a standard item on Fuzhou shop counters and in the tea rooms attached to restaurants. The oolong-making craft of Anxi was inscribed on UNESCO's intangible heritage list in 2022.

How to have it here: It is served gongfu-style rather than in a covered bowl: a small clay pot, 110 ml, about 8 grams of leaf, and pours of 15 ml or less taken in a run of eight to ten rounds. The rolled pellets are dropped whole and never crushed, and each round is timed from the moment the pot is filled, usually 10 seconds for the first and a few seconds longer after that. Because the leaf withstands repeated steeping, a single pot is expected to cover a whole table rather than one cup.

Da Hong Pao (大红袍)

Da Hong Pao is a Wuyi oolong from the cliffs of the Wuyi mountains, one of the teas traditionally reserved for the emperor. It is a semi-fermented rock tea, rolled by hand and fired in stages, and the bushes grow in cliffside gardens in the same mountain range as Lapsang Souchong, roughly 300 km north of Fuzhou.

How to have it here: Rock oolong from the Wuyi cliffs is brewed in a covered bowl or a small gaiwan at 95-100°C, with a first steep of about 15 seconds and 6 to 8 rounds taken in. The leaf is usually sold as a tight twisted strip rather than a pellet, and the grade is marked by the number of infusions it will give, which is why packs from Fuzhou shops tend to list a count rather than a weight alone.

Teahouses

Sanfang Qixiang tea-house quarter (三坊七巷)

Sanfang Qixiang is the largest surviving li-fang block in China, a grid of 3 named lanes and 7 named alleys arranged around the north-south axis of Nanhou Street: Yijin, Wenru and Guanglu to the west, with Yangqiao, Langguan, Ta, Huang, Anmin, Gong and Jibi alleys to the east. The quarter preserves dense Ming and Qing buildings and is described as a museum of that architecture, and it was the traditional residential area of the city's upper classes. Tea houses in the lanes are the places where the local jasmine tea is poured through the day.

Area: Gulou District, Fuzhou

Tenfu Tea (天福茗茶)

Tenfu is a Fujian tea house and tea retail chain built on the Tianren tea business, founded in mainland China in 1993, and it lists the tea-art teahouses 天仁茶艺馆 as its retail format. The parent company was floated in Hong Kong in November 2010 at an offer price of 4.8 to 6.8 HKD per share, with a final price of 6 HKD on 208.62 million new shares, raising up to 1.4 billion HKD. Its head office is the Tenfu Tea Shop at Pantuo in Zhangpu County.

Area: Zhangpu County, Fujian

Jasmine tea etiquette in Fuzhou

The vessel is the gaiwan, a covered bowl of about 150 ml holding roughly 5 grams of leaf. The lid does double duty: held slightly ajar to pour, then lifted to the nose and inhaled before the first sip, because jasmine grades are judged on the scent first. Water around 85-90°C, first steep about 45 seconds, 3 refills as a rule.

Fuzhou pours for the table, not for the individual: the host fills guests' cups first, in age order, and keeps the pot moving. A guest taps 2 fingers when topped up. Filling a cup to the brim is poor form - 7 or 8 tenths full is the local standard - and asking how many scenting rounds a lot took, 3 or 9, is the ordinary way to talk quality.

The covered bowl is set down rather than held while talking, and the person pouring keeps the spout clear of the cup rim so no drips land on the saucer. Poured leaves are never fished out with a spoon at a Fuzhou table; spent leaf is tipped out after 3 or 4 rounds, and a second brewing is offered only if the host asks. Anything scented is treated as a group property, and the label - base tea, scenting count, harvest year - is discussed before the first cup is taken.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does the scenting count on the label mean?

It is the number of times the base green tea was layered with fresh jasmine blossoms - from 3 rounds in standard grades up to 9 in the finest. More rounds mean more flowers and more labour, so the price scales with the count. A 7- or 9-round lot needs several nights of scenting in August, when the jasmine crop peaks, and each round is followed by a drying stage before the next layer goes on. Top lots end with the spent blossoms removed entirely, leaving leaf only in the pack.

Why is Fuzhou associated with jasmine tea?

The city has run commercial scenting since the 19th century, sitting between jasmine-growing fields on the plain and green-tea producers in the hills. The July-August blossom season, the several-night layering method and the evening picking of the flowers all point back to Fuzhou workshops. Local growers have cultivated jasmine for several hundred years and lead China in output and quality, and Fuzhou jasmine tea was registered as a geographical indication in 2008.

Is Lapsang Souchong also from Fuzhou?

No. It is a black tea from Tongmu Village in the Wuyi mountains of northern Fujian, about 300 km from Fuzhou, withered over pine fires since the 17th century. It is stocked in Fuzhou as part of the wider Fujian tea trade, but its origin is the mountain country, not the capital of the province's scenting craft. The name is attested before 1640 and Dutch traders shipped small-leaf black tea from the region to Europe by 1610.

Where can I drink it in Fuzhou?

Teahouses in the Sanfang Qixiang lane quarter serve it in covered bowls, the standard 150 ml vessel with about 5 grams of leaf. The quarter is the largest surviving li-fang block in China, with 3 lanes and 7 alleys around Nanhou Street, and is where the city's jasmine trade is centred. Tea shops across the city grade by the scenting count printed on the pack, and tasting two or three counts side by side before buying is a normal request, not a special favour.

How is Tieguanyin different from the local jasmine?

Jasmine tea is scented green tea with the fragrance of flowers added over several nights, and it is judged on scent. Tieguanyin is a semi-fermented oolong from Anxi County in southern Fujian, judged on body and infusion length, and one pot of its pellets can be brewed eight or ten times. The two are served in different vessels, a 150 ml covered bowl against a 110 ml clay pot, and they take different water temperatures: 85-90°C against the near-boiling water used for the rolled oolong pellets.

What water temperature and timing should I use in Fuzhou?

For jasmine tea, water at about 85-90°C and a first steep of roughly 45 seconds, with 3 refills after it. For Lapsang Souchong, 90-95°C for two to three minutes. Gongfu-style oolong takes far less water per pour, about 15 ml, and 8 grams of leaf in a 110 ml pot. Wuyi rock oolong such as Da Hong Pao is brewed at 95-100°C with a 15-second first steep and 6 to 8 rounds.
