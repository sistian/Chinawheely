Overview: Photography in Xi'an

Xi'an has an unusual photographic asset: a complete medieval city wall you can walk on, at 12 m high and 12 to 14 m wide along the top, in the middle of a modern capital. Around it sits a Byzantine-looking Bell Tower at the centre of a dense old city grid, a Tang brick pagoda that leans, and an excavated hot-spring complex at the foot of a mountain. The light is good here too, because the basin geography gives a very clean sky and the dust haze over the old city often acts as a natural diffuser.

Xi'an City Wall (西安城墙)

The wall was built from 1374 to 1378 under the Hongwu Emperor and encloses about 11.52 square kilometres of the old city, standing 12 m high with a top width of 12 to 14 m, ringed by an 18 m wide moat. What makes it photographable is that you can walk on it, and the western section gives you both the interior of the old city with its tiled roofs and, looking outward, the modern skyline. The best frames are from the ramparts near the South Gate or the southeast corner watchtower, where the wall's curve leads the eye away and the crenellations make a repeating rhythm against the sky.

best light: golden hour · landmark

Getting there: South Gate, South West Gate and North Gate have vehicle access and car parks. Metro Line 2 to South Gate or Line 3 to Yuyangmen for the western ramparts.

  • The wall is 13.74 km around, so pick a section rather than trying to do the whole thing in daylight.
  • Rent a bicycle on the ramparts; the western run between the South Gate and the corner tower is the best stretch and takes about 40 minutes.
  • The crenellations are at their most graphic when the sun is low and slightly behind them, so shoot with the light coming over the top of the wall.

The Bell Tower of Xi'an (钟楼)

The Bell Tower was built in 1384 during the Ming and stands at the exact centre of the old walled city, which is unusual enough that the whole street grid radiates from it. It is a square brick base 8.4 m high carrying a two-storey wooden pavilion with a hipped roof and green glazed tiles, flanked by two corner towers. After dark the timber superstructure is lit separately from the brick base, which creates the classic image of a glowing pavilion floating on a dark mass, with the modern shopping streets around it and the restored Drum Tower 100 m away.

best light: blue hour · landmark

Getting there: Metro Line 2 to Bell Tower station, with the tower directly above the station. The surrounding ring road is heavy with traffic, so shoot from the pedestrianised corner rather than the middle.

  • The two corner pavilions are lower than the main tower and are the more elegant frame if you can find an angle along the wall.
  • The Drum Tower opposite is smaller and less lit, which makes a good secondary element in a wider night frame.
  • The intersection is busy at all hours, so a slightly longer exposure will separate the traffic light trails from the pedestrians.

Big Wild Goose Pagoda (大雁塔)

The pagoda was built in 652 by the monk Xuanzang to house the Buddhist scriptures he brought back from India, originally as a five-storey structure 60 m tall, and rebuilt to the present seven-storey brick pavilion form. It now stands 64.1 m in total on a base 4.2 m high with a 4.8 m north-south width, and its lowest tier measures 25 m per side. The tilt is famous: by 1996 it had reached 1.0105 m before conservation work. The north square around it, 300 m by 450 m, contains a statue of Xuanzang, and in the evening the fountain throws water up in front of the pagoda.

best light: golden hour · landmark

Getting there: Metro Line 3 and Line 4 to Big Wild Goose Pagoda station. A car can reach the square on the south side but the area is heavily controlled during evening peak times.

  • The fountain in the north square is the frame; shoot from low down so the water jets cross the pagoda rather than sitting below it.
  • The leaning of the tower is only visible from a specific angle along the south face, so walk the base to find it.
  • Evening, when the tower is lit warm against a deep blue sky, is when the surrounding squares are busiest with people.

Terracotta Army Pit 1 (兵马俑一号坑)

Photographing the army is a problem of artificial light and depth rather than outdoor light, and Pit 1 is the one to work on. The main gallery is roofed and lit by a combination of overhead and side lighting, and the trench itself is 230 m long and 62 m wide with figures standing in twelve earthen corridors separated by tamped partitions with a ceiling of timber beams and brick. The correct shot is from the visitor walkway along the north side, using a wide lens to take in the ranks in perspective, with the excavated crossbow formation as the near foreground.

best light: all day · landmark

Getting there: Inside the Lintong visitor complex, a set of free electric shuttles runs between the three pits and the other halls. The visitor complex is 40 km east of Xi'an.

  • Most people photograph the reconstructed figures rather than the excavated ranks, so the wide gallery perspective is the less crowded shot.
  • Tripods are restricted in some halls; a wide lens at a slower shutter on a low wall is the practical alternative.
  • The Pit 3 figures have survived with more original pigment, so they are the ones worth a close frame and a longer lens.

Huaqing Pool Excavations (华清池遗址)

The excavated hot-spring complex at Huaqing Palace is the most atmospheric ruin in the region, because the eight Tang bathing pools are still fed by live springs and the site sits under the pine-covered slope of Mount Li. The stone is a warm sandstone-coloured material and the channels between the pools are narrow and geometric, which gives strong graphic lines against the organic rock of the hillside. Late afternoon light rakes across the pool edges, and the vapour rising off the hottest pools in the cool early evening air catches it. The Long Pool has the best combination of open water and architectural edging.

best light: golden hour · landmark

Getting there: In Lintong District, 30 km east of Xi'an and 10 km from the Terracotta Army. There is a large visitor car park and an internal shuttle.

  • Late afternoon or early evening gives the best light and also the coolest temperatures in the exposed stone courtyards.
  • The Consort Yang's Pool is the most famous, but the Star Pool and the Long Pool are quieter and give better frames.
  • Stay on the marked paths; the paving around the pools is original and slippery when wet.

The Stele Forest and Confucius Temple (西安碑林)

The Stele Forest began when Bi Yuan, the governor of Shaanxi during the Qianlong reign, collected inscriptions from roadsides and arranged them in rows in front of the government school, and it grew into the largest collection of steles and stone carvings in China, now housed in the former Confucius Temple. The photographic interest comes from the raking light on the stone, from the repetition of the stele rows, and from the contrast between the severe grey slabs and the timber of the surrounding halls. The 2000 archaeological study of the steles identified the Nestorian Christian text recording the religion's arrival in China in 635.

best light: all day · street-life

Getting there: Metro Line 2 to Yongningmen station, then a short walk north on Wenmiao Street. There is limited parking on the surrounding lanes.

  • The best-lit galleries are the covered walkways, where the light rakes across the carved faces of the steles.
  • The famous calligraphic steles are in the second and third courtyards, and a longer lens lets you fill the frame with a single character column.
  • Soft overcast light is ideal here, since it avoids the glare that polished stone surfaces throw back at the camera.

The Tang Paradise and Datang Everbright City (大唐芙蓉园)

Tang Paradise opened on 11 April 2005 in the Qujiang New District on the site of the Tang imperial Furong Garden, covering 1,000 mu of which the Furong Lake water is 300 mu, and it was planned from Tang architectural and garden principles rather than being a literal reconstruction. The Datang Everbright City, a pedestrian street 2,100 m long and 500 m wide running from the Tang city wall site up to the south plaza of the Big Wild Goose Pagoda, was begun in 2002 and remodelled by the city government in 2018. After dark the sequence of illuminated Tang-style buildings reads as a film set.

best light: blue hour · night

Getting there: Metro Line 4 to Datang Furongyuan station. The pedestrian street is closed to traffic, so a car gets you only to the edge.

  • Shoot along the axis with the pagoda in the distance; the perspective of the two kilometres of street is what makes the frame.
  • The Furong Lake side has traditional covered walkways and water reflections that are quieter than the main avenue.
  • It is one of China's first national demonstration pedestrian streets, designated in 2020.

Working with the Xi'an basin light

The Xi'an basin traps dust and haze, which is usually a nuisance and here is an advantage. The fine suspended particles scatter the low sun into a warm glow that flatters the brick of the city wall and the Tang masonry, and they give long shadows without harsh contrast. The worst case is a hard clear afternoon, when the light is white and flat and the sky goes pale blue.

Because the light is so soft and the surfaces so textured, the highest-value technique here is patience with detail work rather than wide spectacular landscapes. The carved characters on the Stele Forest, the brick vaulting of the Terracotta Army pits and the pool edges at Huaqing all reward a good macro or a tight telephoto.

For the night work, the tower illumination on the Bell Tower, the Wild Goose Pagoda and the Datang Everbright City is bright enough to shoot handheld at moderate ISO, but a small tripod still helps with the fountain in the Wild Goose Pagoda square, where you want the water to blur.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the Xi'an City Wall worth walking, and how long does it take?

Yes, and it is the defining experience of the city. The wall encloses 11.52 square kilometres, is 12 m high, and its top is 12 to 14 m wide, so you can walk a substantial section comfortably. It runs 13.74 km in total, which is more than most visitors want. The best two hours are the western section between the South Gate and the southeast corner tower, where the ramparts curve away from you and you look out over the tiled roofs of the old city.

How do I photograph the Terracotta Army when the pits are roofed and dim?

Use the pit galleries' own light rather than fighting it. Pit 1 is 230 m long with figures in twelve corridors, and the standard shot from the north visitor walkway with a wide lens gives the ranks in perspective. Bring a wide lens, shoot with the ISO up rather than the shutter down if a tripod is not permitted, and go to Pit 3 for the close-up detail, since the figures there have retained the most original pigment.

What is the best time of day at the Big Wild Goose Pagoda?

Late afternoon into evening. The pagoda is 64.1 m tall on a 4.2 m base with a 4.8 m north-south width and its lowest tier 25 m per side, and in the low warm light the brick takes on a colour that flat midday cannot produce. The north square's fountain is at its best in the blue hour after the sky darkens, when the lit water catches the tower behind it. The tower's own 1 metre lean is visible from a specific spot along the south face.

Can I get a clean image of the Bell Tower with no traffic?

Not completely, because the tower sits on a busy five-way intersection, but you can minimise it. The tower is lit differently at the base and the timber pavilion, and the best approach is from one of the pedestrianised corners at a slight distance so the traffic light trails and moving taxis are separated from the structure. A 2 to 4 second exposure at dusk will blur the vehicles into soft streaks while the tower stays sharp.

Which Xi'an photo spot works best in bad weather?

The Stele Forest. The covered walkways between the stele courtyards are sheltered, the light is diffuse, and the subject is the calligraphy itself, which is unaffected by a flat grey sky. The Terracotta Army pits are the other reliable choice for poor weather, because they are all indoors with controlled lighting and the camera can be set for whatever the conditions are.

Are there drone restrictions over the Xi'an City Wall?

Yes, flying over the wall and the historic city centre is restricted, and the wall itself is a national heritage structure with strict protection. The broader urban area also has controlled airspace near the airport to the west. The wall ramparts themselves give you a raised perspective without any of that, and at 12 m above the old city roofs they are a better vantage than most drone shots would have been.
