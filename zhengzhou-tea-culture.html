Overview: Tea Culture in Zhengzhou

Zhengzhou is the provincial capital of Henan and the assembly point for the province's tea trade, which is dominated by a single leaf: Xinyang Maojian, the green tea made in the southwestern mountains of Xinyang, about 300 kilometres to the south of the city. The capital also carries a distinct kind of tea house life of its own, the opera teahouse, where as of 2007 the city held more than 20 of the 100-plus such houses in Henan.

The tea

Xinyang Maojian (信阳毛尖)

Xinyang Maojian is a green tea from the southwestern mountains of Xinyang in Henan, made in areas such as Cheyun Mountain, Lianyun Mountain, Jiyun Mountain, Tianyun Mountain, Yunwu Mountain, Bailongtan, Heilongtan and Hejiazhai. It is named for its slender, straight, downy buds, and is styled on fine, round, glossy, straight leaves with a high aroma and a long sweet aftertaste. It took a gold medal at the 1915 San Francisco world's fair, was named one of the ten most famous teas of China in 1959, and was rated a national famous tea by the Ministry of Commerce in 1982, 1986 and 1990.

How to have it here: It is brewed in a covered glass or porcelain vessel at 85 to 90 degrees Celsius, with 3 grams to about 150 millilitres of water, and the leaf is usually not rinsed because the down on the tips is the part that gives the liquor its clarity. Zhengzhou offices and hotels typically serve it plain, and the spring picking from the last week of March is the lot that carries the highest price in the year's list.

Brick tea (砖茶)

Brick tea is compressed into blocks for long-distance transport and is the traditional tea of northern China and Inner Mongolia. The blocks are pressed in moulds of different patterns, wrapped in oiled paper and carried over long distances, the tea of the Central Plains being sent west towards Tibet and to Xinjiang along the Tea Horse Road. Fu brick tea is produced in Shaanxi, black brick and flower brick tea in Hunan, and Sichuan remains a main producing area, where brick tea is made from black tea.

How to have it here: A piece is broken off the block and boiled rather than steeped, which is why brick tea appears in northern households and in the Hui communities of the region rather than on a southern-style tea tray. In Zhengzhou it is the tea for long sessions and cold weather, and it is the base for the pressed cakes and bricks that arrive from Yunnan and are traded through the capital's tea shops.

Jasmine tea (茉莉花茶)

Jasmine tea is scented rather than fermented: green tea is layered with fresh jasmine blossoms over several nights in Fuzhou, so the scent is absorbed rather than added. The flowers are picked in the evening, when they are at their strongest. It is a processed tea of the flower-tea class, and the making of Fuzhou jasmine tea is itself recorded as an intangible cultural heritage item.

How to have it here: The northern way with jasmine is a heavy brew in a lidded porcelain pot, poured into small cups that are topped up repeatedly through a morning of business. It is the standard everyday tea in Zhengzhou offices and at the counters of the city's tea dealers, and it is the base used for the scented blends sold alongside the local Maojian.

Teahouses

Opera teahouses (戏曲茶楼)

Zhengzhou's distinct tea house is the opera teahouse, where a stage performance and a pot of tea are the same transaction. Opera is a mass art in Henan, with Yuju, Quju and Yuetiao among the provincial repertoire and more than 10 other local forms, and performers have moved out of the theatre into the tea houses where they work among the audience rather than before it. By 2007 Henan counted more than 100 opera teahouses and Zhengzhou held more than 20 of them, the largest share of any city in the province. Performances run on the tea houses rather than in purpose-built opera theatres, and the city's radio stations carry related programming for the same audience.

Drinking and buying tea in Zhengzhou

The city is a buying market as much as a drinking one, and the trade in Maojian follows the leaf up through the grades. The top grade is picked as 1 bud with 1 opening leaf, and it takes more than 100,000 buds to make 1 kilogram of finished tea; the first grade uses 1 bud with 2 leaves, the second and third grades 1 bud with 2 to 3 leaves, and the fourth and fifth grades 1 bud with 3 leaves. Fresh leaf is spread out for 4 to 10 hours at a thickness of about 3 centimetres, and the day's leaf is fired the same day.

Firing is a three-stage operation and the numbers are fixed. In the raw pan, at 160 to 200 degrees Celsius, a charge of about 500 grams is turned for 3 to 5 minutes and then shaped for about 7 minutes, until the leaf reaches 4 or 5 parts dryness; the finished pan, at 80 to 100 degrees, is where the fine, round, glossy and straight form of the tea is set, ending at 7 or 8 parts dryness. Drying follows twice, first over charcoal at 70 to 90 degrees down to about 15 percent moisture, then at around 60 degrees down to below 6 percent.

For the drinker the rule is that Maojian is not rinsed and not over-boiled. Water at 85 to 90 degrees is poured over 3 grams of leaf in a covered vessel and left for 3 minutes, and a second infusion can be taken from the same leaf. The buyer's habit is to read the grade, the picking date and the drying figures on the label before the price, and the 1999 Kunming exposition gold and the 2007 gold in Japan are the 2 awards most often quoted.

Frequently Asked Questions

How far is the Xinyang tea country from Zhengzhou?

About 300 kilometres to the south, across the province and into the Dabie Mountains. Xinyang itself covers 18,916 square kilometres with about 8.85 million residents, sits on the upper Huai River, and borders Anhui's Lu'an to the north and Hubei's Huanggang and Xiaogan to the south, which places the growing area 2 to 3 days of road travel from the capital.

What did Xinyang Maojian win at the 1915 fair?

A gold medal at the 1915 San Francisco world's fair, the exhibition held for the Panama-Pacific celebration, which is the earliest international award recorded for the tea. The tea's international standing was later confirmed in China: named one of the ten most famous teas in 1959, rated a national famous tea by the Ministry of Commerce in 1982, 1986 and 1990, awarded a state silver quality prize in 1985 and a gold quality prize in 1990, and given gold medals at the 1999 Kunming world horticultural exposition and the 2007 world green tea conference in Japan.

How old is the tea history of the Xinyang area?

The Xinyang tea district is one of the old tea districts of China and its production is generally traced back to the Eastern Zhou, with large-scale production under the Tang. It was called da mo tea in the Tang, called sheet tea and loose tea in the Song, bud tea and leaf tea in the Ming, and took the name Xinyang Maojian only in the Qing. Between about 760 and 780 the tea writer Lu Yu, in the first monograph on tea in the world, placed Xinyang in the Huainan tea district, one of the 8 great tea regions of his time.

What makes the leaf of Maojian worth grading so carefully?

The quality of the fresh leaf decides the finished tea. A well-tempered growing area gives a bright, oily leaf, while poor humidity gives a dry, dull one, and leaf picked too early is fragrant but thin and low in yield while old, coarse leaf is weak in aroma. The leaf is therefore sorted both coarsely and finely, by picking season, size, colour, weight, thickness and tenderness, following the grading table for Xinyang Maojian fresh leaf.

What is the tea house scene in Zhengzhou like?

It is built on opera rather than on refreshment alone. Henan is a province of opera, with more than 10 local forms beyond Yuju, Quju and Yuetiao, and the performers work inside the tea houses rather than on theatre stages, moving among the tables. As of 2007 the province had more than 100 opera teahouses and Zhengzhou counted more than 20 of them, and the city's parks and cultural palaces have become the main gathering places for the performers and their audiences.

What other teas do people drink in Zhengzhou?

Brick tea, the compressed block carried over long distances and boiled rather than steeped, is the traditional tea of northern China, and it is the form that the tea of the Central Plains travelled west in, towards Tibet and Xinjiang along the Tea Horse Road. Jasmine tea, scented in Fuzhou over several nights of fresh blossoms, is the everyday table tea of the capital's offices and tea counters, and pressed cakes and bricks from Yunnan pass through the city's shops as well.
