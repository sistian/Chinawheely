Best photo spots in Beijing — from iconic landmarks to hidden gems. Complete photography guide.

Overview: Photography in Beijing

Beijing's photographic reputation rests on two things, and neither is what most visitors shoot. The first is the axis: a city laid out on a north-south line with the Forbidden City at its centre and the Temple of Heaven to the south, so that the same low sun can put you at both. The second is the wall, which changes completely with the weather. The rest is a city of hutong courtyards and of wide modern avenues, and both reward a patient walk rather than a single famous viewpoint.

The Forbidden City (故宫博物院)

The Forbidden City was built from 1406 to 1420 with more than a million workers, covers 72 hectares inside a 961 by 753 m rectangle, and is surrounded by a 52 m wide, 6 m deep moat with walls 7.9 m high. That moat is the photograph most people get wrong. Shot from the north-west corner bridge just after sunrise, with the wall running away diagonally and the corner tower at the far end, it becomes a graphic of warm grey brick and dark water, and the moat's width gives you the depth that the axis views lack. The unvisited version is the western palace precincts, far quieter than the three central courtyards.

best light: sunrise · landmark

Getting there: The Meridian Gate on the south side is the main entrance, and it opens at 8:30 with the ticket released online the previous evening. Line 1 to Tian'anmen West or Line 8 to Shichahai and walk east. A car cannot enter the complex; use the drop-off on the north side at Beihai.

  • The north-west corner of the moat, near the corner tower, gives the strongest diagonal and is reached through the Beihai side gate.
  • The plaza in front of the Meridian Gate is enormous, so a long lens is necessary to compress it and separate the gate from the mass of visitors.
  • Late afternoon light on the western palace roofs through the Gate of Divine Might is the alternative to the crowded morning axis.

Great Wall at Badaling (八达岭长城)

The walkable section at Badaling runs 3,741 m with an average height over 7 m and some sections at 14 m, and it crosses ridge after ridge, which is why it works photographically from almost any point along it. The strongest frame looks along the wall rather than across it, so the crenellations and stepped stone path run away from the camera into the mountains, with far ridges in haze behind. Sunrise is best because the light rakes along the wall, picking out the texture of the brick, and the tour buses have not yet arrived. The 700 m ground cable car saves the climb, so you can be on the wall by 7 am.

best light: sunrise · nature

Getting there: The cable car lower station is opposite the Zhan Tianyou memorial hall in the north of the visitor centre, 80 km northwest of central Beijing. The Badaling branch of Metro Line 1 reaches the area with a shuttle bus to the car park.

  • Shoot from the wall itself, slightly off the crest, so the wall fills the lower frame and the ridge line leads away.
  • Wait for a shaft of light to break through the haze rather than shooting the whole ridge flat.
  • The North Line is quieter than the South Line and gives more open ridge and fewer buildings in the background.

Temple of Heaven (天坛)

The Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests is a circular building 36 m in diameter and 38 m tall on three tiers of marble, with a triple-gabled blue glazed tile roof, standing at the north end of a 267 hectare complex. The best time is not tourist midday but the blue hour before dawn, when the glazed roof takes on a deep saturated blue against a lighter sky and the old cypress trees behind are still dark silhouettes. The building is also lit from the south, so from the Circular Mound Altar you get the full elevation of the roof and the three tiers read clearly.

best light: blue hour · landmark

Getting there: The East Gate is on the east side of the complex, reached from Line 5 to Tiantandongmen. The park itself opens at 6 am, before the relic sites at 8 am, and the South Gate is nearest the Circular Mound Altar.

  • The park is free and open from 6 am, while the three main relic sites charge separately and close at 17:30 in summer.
  • Stand on the Circular Mound Altar, which is higher and directly south, for the full elevation of the Hall of Prayer.
  • Old cypress trees over a thousand years old stand behind the Hall, and they make a dark frame in the pre-dawn light.

Beihai Park and the White Pagoda (北海公园)

Beihai covers 70 hectares with more than half of it water, and it is the oldest imperial garden in the world, begun in the Liao and Jin dynasties and carrying the one-pool-three-mountains layout that China's imperial gardens copied. The 35.9 m White Pagoda on Jade Flower Island was built in 1651 at the request of a Tibetan lama. The photograph that works is from the western shore in the last hour of light, with the pagoda silhouetted against a coloured sky and reflected in still water, framed by the ferry traffic and the willows. From the north bank you get a completely different composition.

best light: sunset · golden-hour

Getting there: The north gate on Di'anmen West Street opens earliest, and the south gate by the Bridge of Ten Thousand Years connects to the Beihai side of the Forbidden City. Line 6 to Beihai West or Line 8 to Shichahai. The park car park is on Wenjin Street.

  • The western shore between the Five-Dragon Pavilion and the ferry landing is the classic sunset position for the pagoda.
  • A small island boat runs across to Jade Flower Island, which puts you in the middle of the reflection rather than at its edge.
  • The Jade Flower Island Temple of the Ten Thousand Buddhas at the base of the pagoda has fine gilt wood carving that photographs well in low light.

798 Art District (798艺术区)

798 occupies a former East German electronics plant built with Soviet reparations money under the First Five Year Plan, with 1.1 million square metres of Bauhaus factory sheds that look startlingly un-Chinese. The 718 United Plant was designed by East German engineers, and the factory closed in 1964 before artists moved in from 2002. The subject is the industrial architecture itself: sawtooth roof monitors, bare concrete, painted steel staircases and external fire escapes. The outdoor galleries are free, the light inside the sheds comes through the roof monitors in hard bars, and late afternoon turns the shed walls warm while the interiors stay blue and dim.

best light: golden hour · street-life

Getting there: The complex is in the Jiuxianqiao area of Chaoyang District, with subway stations at Gaobeidian and Tuofangying on Line 14. Parking is in the internal lots. The outdoor galleries are free; the main galleries charge.

  • The roof monitors create hard shafts of light in the middle of the day and warm washes along the shed walls in the late afternoon.
  • Shoot the sawtooth roof profile against the sky from the elevated walkway for a clean graphic frame.
  • Weekday mornings give you the sheds without the visitor traffic and the gallery openings.

Yonghe Temple (雍和宫)

Yonghe was a Qing princely residence converted into a lamasery in 1744, and it is the largest and most complete Tibetan Buddhist monastery in China proper, covering 64,000 square metres with five courtyards and 900 rooms. The building that photographs best is the 26 m tall Maitreya Pavilion, a solid timber structure on a white marble base with a gilded copper roof and rows of small buddhas around the eaves, lit from below after dark. The night scene is the reason to come, because incense smoke drifts across the courtyard and catches the floodlights, and the gold roof reads as a separate element against the deep blue evening.

best light: blue hour · night

Getting there: Line 2 and Line 5 to Yonghegong station, with the gate directly at the exit. It is 4 km from the city centre and the lanes around it, particularly the Guozijian Street side, are among the quietest in central Beijing.

  • The Yonghegong station exit opens onto the gate, so this is one of the easiest sites in Beijing to reach without a car.
  • Evening, when the forecourt is busy with worshippers and the incense smoke catches the light, is the strongest time.
  • Walk the Guozijian Street side to get the monastery walls and old trees without the main gate crowd.

The Temple of Heaven Park and the Long Corridor (天坛公园)

The 267 hectare park around the Temple of Heaven is one of the largest urban parks in China and it is used in the early morning by people doing tai chi, choral singing, ballroom dancing and playing instruments, which makes it the best free street photography in Beijing. The subject is the older people's morning rather than the monuments. The Long Corridor, the 728 m covered walkway leading to the Hall of Prayer, gives a compressed perspective with painted ceiling panels running away in one-point perspective, and in late afternoon the low sun comes down its length and lights the beams from the side.

best light: golden hour · street-life

Getting there: The East Gate at Tiantandongmen is closest to the metro, and the West Gate opens onto the old cypress grove. The park is free from 6 am and the perimeter is several kilometres, so a car parked at a gate lets you start at one end and walk out at another.

  • Arrive between 6 and 8 am for the morning exercise groups, and ask permission before photographing individuals at close range.
  • The Long Corridor runs south-north, so the low evening sun enters along its length and lights the ceiling panels.
  • The old cypress trees around the west gate are over 300 years old and their trunks are thicker than a person can span.

Light, haze and the Beijing axis

Beijing's air carries a fine dust that scatters short wavelengths, which is why the light here is warmer and lower-contrast than in coastal cities, and why the classic golden hour is longer than the latitude would suggest. The practical consequence is that the best light in Beijing is soft and directional rather than hard, and that the hour after sunrise and the hour before sunset are both worth shooting in full. The 7 am light on the Badaling wall and the 5 pm light on the Forbidden City walls are the same quality of light at opposite ends of the day.

The moat and lake water in Beijing give you reflections at all times of day, which is a reason to prioritise early starts. The 52 m wide moat around the Forbidden City, the water at Beihai, the moat at the Summer Palace and the Kunming Lake all hold a reflection best when the wind is low, which in this climate means before 9 am.

For the crowded sites, a lens strategy matters more than a body. A 200 mm compresses the Forbidden City's axis and separates the corner towers from the mass of visitors, and it isolates the laughing Buddha and the smaller cliff carvings at Mutianyu. At the 798 art district, by contrast, a 24 to 35 mm wide angle is the tool, because the subject is architecture and depth rather than a distant subject.

Frequently Asked Questions: Booking in Beijing

What is the best time of day for the Forbidden City?

At opening, 8:30 am, for two reasons. The crowds have not built up, and the light comes in from the east across the courtyard axis, so the main halls are lit from the side rather than flat. If you want the classic axis shots with people in them, arrive by 9 am and work from the Meridian Gate looking north. The moat and the north-west corner bridge are better photographed before 8 am, when you can be on site ahead of the ticketing opening.

Is Badaling better than Mutianyu for photography?

Badaling has more dramatic open ridge and a summit, but Mutianyu has better-preserved detail, woodland on every approach and far fewer people. Photographically the deciding factor is the density: at Badaling the wall itself is often crowded while the frames across the valley are empty, whereas at Mutianyu the whole scene is quiet. If you can get to Badaling before 8 am, the ridge light is the best in the region.

How do I photograph the Temple of Heaven without thousands of people?

Go to the park at 6 am, before the relic sites open at 8 am and before the tour coaches arrive. The park itself is free, and the pre-dawn blue hour is when the Hall of Prayer's glazed roof reads best against the sky. Then return for the 8 am opening, when the surrounding plazas are full of residents doing tai chi, choral groups and ballroom dancing, which is the street photography the park is famous for.

Which Beijing site works best in rain or haze?

Beihai Park. The 35.9 m White Pagoda on Jade Flower Island is lit from below and reads strongly even in flat light, and the lake holds a reflection in almost any weather. The 798 art district is the other good choice, because the hard shafts of light through the sawtooth roof monitors only exist in poor weather. In heavy smog, Beihai's pagoda is one of the few subjects in the city that stays legible.

Do I need to book the Forbidden City in advance?

Yes, and increasingly so. Tickets are released online through the official Palace Museum channels a few days ahead and sell out quickly in peak season, with the limit capped at 40,000 visitors a day. Booking the entry slot in advance is essential for any plan that depends on a specific arrival time, since the released tickets are timed. The park around the Temple of Heaven, by contrast, has no ticketing and no gate.

Are there good street photography spots beyond the famous monuments?

Yes, and they are cheaper and quieter. The Temple of Heaven park at 6 am is the strongest free subject in the city, filled with people doing tai chi, choral singing and ballroom dancing. The lanes behind Yonghe Temple towards Guozijian Street, the hutongs around Shichahai, and the weathered courtyard walls of the older neighbourhoods all give you working street scenes with none of the ticket queue.
