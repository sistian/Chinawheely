Overview: Eating and Shopping in Xi'an

Xi'an's street kitchen is a register of wheat, lamb and pork cooked in large iron pots and handed over in pieces, not slices. Bread is torn by hand, soup is poured over it at the table, and the sequence of eating is part of the dish. This page covers six foods and four areas to shop, with founding dates, district counts and measured dimensions taken from the record.

What to eat

Roujiamo, meat in flatbread (肉夹馍)

Sliced braised pork tucked into a baijimo flatbread baked in a clay oven or over charcoal. The meat is cooked for hours in a large pot with a thick layer of fat on the surface, which sets pale like wax and gives the name la zhi rou. 2 documented styles exist: the la zhi rou version on a baijimo, and the Xifeng meat mince version from Baoji with vinegar added.

Where: Everywhere in the old city, with the highest concentration of counters around the Muslim Quarter; the Tang-style variant is often sold near the south gate of the city wall.

Biangbiang wide noodles (biangbiang面)

Hand-pulled belt-like noodles from the Guanzhong region, made by kneading, stretching and slapping the dough, so wide the strips read as trousers belts — the first of the Eight Curiosities of Shaanxi. The name is written with a character of 58 strokes in its traditional form, an onomatopoeia for the sound of dough hitting the work surface. Before chilli oil, the sheets are served plain or with a meat sauce.

Where: Street-front noodle shops through the old town, most of them a short walk from the Bell Tower or inside the Muslim Quarter.

Lamb soup with torn bread (羊肉泡馍)

Pieces of a flatbread called tuotuo mo, baked from an unleavened dough, soaked in a mutton or beef broth made with bone, fennel and star anise. The bread is broken to thumb-sized pieces by the diner, then simmered in the concentrated stock for one or two minutes and finished with lamb fat. 4 broth levels exist, from a single sip left in the bowl to the fullest service.

Where: Old eateries across the old city, with the densest cluster in the Muslim Quarter, where beef versions and the pork-intestine variant Hulutou are also served.

Hulutou intestine soup (葫芦头)

The Xi'an version of paomo made with pork: torn pieces of a lightly leavened flatbread in a bowl with 4 toppings: sliced pork large intestine, vermicelli, soaked wood ear and dried tofu, flooded repeatedly with a white broth simmered from pork bones, pork stomach and chicken. The name refers to the fatty segment where the large and small intestines meet. The flatbread carries a little leavening.

Where: Old eateries in the Muslim Quarter and the north side of the city, a staple of the city's morning and midday hours.

Zenggao, glutinous rice and date cake (甑糕)

A Guanzhong sweet breakfast of glutinous rice and red dates, steamed in a zeng and layered 3 or 4 deep, with jujube, rice and azuki beans built up in that order and a final layer of large red dates on top. The pan is steamed for 4 to 6 hours. In October 2018 the Xi'an bureau issued local production standards for zenggao and 7 other traditional snacks.

Where: Breakfast stalls and market counters through the Guanzhong plain, sold in square portions cut from the steamer.

Jia San stuffed soup buns (贾三灌汤包)

The Xi'an Muslim Quarter's version of the soup bun, filled with Qin River beef and grassland lamb, with the broth made from beef and sheep bone marrow simmered slowly rather than from pork jelly as in the northern original. The wider dish dates to the Northern Song capital of Bianjing, where a shop called Yulou listed a version among 72 famous establishments.

Where: Jia San's counters inside the Muslim Quarter, along Beiyuanmen and the lanes off Huajue Lane.

Where to shop

Muslim Quarter (回民街)

The large Muslim neighbourhood inside the city wall in the northwest quarter, bounded by West Street to the north, Beiyuanmen to the west, Hongbu Street to the south and Zaoci Lane to the east, covering 4 communities including Huajue Lane and Saji Bridge. A 2003 count recorded 32,361 Muslims, 49.8 per cent of the city's total. The old 'seven mosques and thirteen wards' pattern became 9 after 1920.

Where: Northwest inside the Xi'an city wall, around Huajue Lane, Daxuexi Lane and Saji Bridge.

Bell Tower of Xi'an (钟楼)

Built in 1384 in the seventeenth year of the Hongwu reign, standing on the axis of the Imperial Way at the crossing of the four main streets. The brick-and-timber tower is close to 40 metres high on a square base covering 1,377 square metres. The Tang Jingyun bell it housed was moved to the Stele Forest Museum in 1953 and a replica installed in 1997.

Where: Centre of the walled city, at the intersection of East, West, South and North Streets.

Big Wild Goose Pagoda and Daci'en Temple (大雁塔)

A seven-storey square brick pagoda in the Daci'en Temple complex, begun in 652 under the monk Xuanzang to house the Sanskrit manuscripts and images he brought back from India. The original five-storey tower was rebuilt under Wu Zetian and repaired by later dynasties, with the present brick facing largely the work of the Ming. Tablets by the south entrance carry texts by Emperor Taizong and Emperor Gaozong.

Where: Yanta District, south of the walled city, on the southern approach road.

Xi'an City Wall (西安城墙)

One of the oldest and best preserved Chinese city walls, built under the Hongwu Emperor in the 14th century and later restored three times, in the late 1500s, the late 1700s and 1983. The enclosed area measures about 11.52 square kilometres. It was designated among China's first major national historical and cultural sites in March 1961.

Where: The wall ring encircling the old city, with the major gates at the south, north, east and west.

Eating and shopping in Xi'an: practical notes

Two rules apply to paomo. First, break the bread yourself if the shop allows it — the texture you produce is part of the dish, and diners who want it uniform can ask for the pre-cut version. Second, choose your broth level before you start: 'mouth soup' leaves a sip at the bottom, 'dry soak' leaves no liquid at all, and the fullest service floods the bowl. Chopsticks cannot be set down in the bowl, since a dry soak leaves nowhere to rest them.

For shopping, treat the walled city as the venue and the Bell Tower as its centre. The Muslim Quarter's lanes are narrow and congest easily, so plan to walk rather than drive, and go early in the day to meet the food vendors at full stock. Around the wall, the covered lanes carry the ordinary retail trade of the neighbourhood — spice, tea, hardware, cloth and paper goods — in long runs rather than malls.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Xi'an's most famous street food?

Roujiamo, sliced braised pork in a flatbread, described in the Chinese sources as the city's version of a hamburger and traditionally dated to the Warring States period. Lamb-and-bread paomo and the belt-wide biangbiang noodles are the other two dishes every list begins with.

What does the character in biangbiang mean?

The traditional form has 58 strokes and cannot be typed easily, so it is usually written out as the sound 'biang'. It is generally treated as an onomatopoeia for the noise thick dough makes when it is slapped on the work surface, though several rival explanations exist, including a derivation from a local word for flatbread.

How is paomo actually eaten?

The diner breaks the tuotuo bread into pieces about the size of a thumb and hands them to the kitchen, which simmers them in concentrated stock with sliced meat and a little vermicelli for one to two minutes before finishing with lamb fat. Sugar garlic, chilli paste and coriander are added at the table, and the amount of broth determines which of the four traditional names applies.

Why is the Muslim Quarter worth visiting beyond the food?

It is the city's main Muslim residential quarter inside the wall, a district of narrow lanes with a documented mosque history running from seven mosques and thirteen wards in the Qing through nine after 1920 to twelve after 1982. A 2003 survey counted 32,361 Muslim residents, 49.8 per cent of the city's Muslim population.

Which structures stand inside the walled city?

The Bell Tower, built in 1384 and standing nearly 40 metres on a base of 1,377 square metres, and the Big Wild Goose Pagoda in the Daci'en Temple complex, begun in 652 to store the texts Xuanzang brought back from India and now seven storeys high after a Wu Zetian-era rebuild and Ming repairs. The pagoda was listed as a national protected site in 1961 and as a Silk Roads World Heritage component in 2014.
