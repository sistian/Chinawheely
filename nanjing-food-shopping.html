Overview: Eating and Shopping in Nanjing

Nanjing served as capital to ten regimes from 229 AD onward, and its kitchen kept two registers: refined duck butchery for court tables and cheap, filling snacks for dock and factory workers. This page logs six dishes with dated origins and four retail blocks with recorded histories, enough to plan a food-and-shopping loop inside the old city wall without leaning on hearsay.

What to eat

Salted duck (盐水鸭)

The city's signature cold dish: ducks dry-cured with salt and star anise, steeped in aged brine, then poached and pressed. Written records of brined duck in Nanjing reach back to the Six Dynasties (3rd–6th century), and the craft standardized under the Ming. Autumn birds carry the alternate name osmanthus duck; one bird carves into 20–30 strips.

Where: Every braised-meat counter in the old city; the Fuzimiao quarter and Laomendong lanes hold the densest cluster of long-standing brands.

Duck blood vermicelli soup (鸭血粉丝汤)

A late-20th-century invention now eaten daily: cubes of set duck blood, sliced gizzard and liver, mung-bean vermicelli and tofu puffs in a white duck broth, finished with white pepper and cilantro. Bowls surfaced at Nanjing stalls in the 1980s and franchised nationwide after 2000. A standard bowl carries roughly 100 grams of noodles, blood and offal together.

Where: Stalls and chains across all districts; the Fuzimiao riverfront and Xinjiekou side lanes keep the longest hours.

Nanjing soup dumplings (汤包)

Thin-skinned pork dumplings built around aspic: the jelly melts into soup during a roughly 10-minute steam, so diners lift each piece onto a spoon, nip the skin, drink first, then dip in black vinegar with ginger shreds. The format was popularized by cooks at the Jiming restaurant in the 1950s; one basket holds 6–8 dumplings.

Where: Jiming Tangbao branches citywide; independent dumpling houses cluster off Xinjiekou and around Gulou.

Qijiawan beef pasties (牛肉锅贴)

Halal pan-fried pasties from a Hui neighborhood over 600 years old: crescents of wheat dough sealed around seasoned beef and scallion, fried base-down in flat pans until one side turns brittle. Served 8–10 per plate with vinegar and chilli dip; beef replaces the pork used elsewhere because the quarter keeps Hui dietary law.

Where: The original Qijiawan lane sits off Shengzhou Road in Jianye District; eponymous branches operate across the city.

Pidu noodles (皮肚面)

A one-bowl lunch from the 1980s factory era: alkaline wheat noodles in pork-bone broth under pidu — pork skin dried, then deep-fried until it rehydrates spongy — plus quail egg, greens, sausage and liver. A 'small' bowl still stacks six to eight toppings; everything is dumped onto the noodles and stirred at the table.

Where: Old-city noodle shops all day; Mingwalang alley near Xinjiekou queues through the 11:30–13:00 lunch rush.

Sweet taro in osmanthus syrup (桂花糖芋苗)

A Qinhuai dessert soup: thumb-sized taro cubes stewed with alkali until the flesh reddens, thickened with fermented-rice paste and dried osmanthus, sweetened and served warm in bowls of eight to ten cubes. It has sold on the Fuzimiao stall rows since at least the Republican era and travels with red-bean rice-ball soup on the same menus.

Where: Sweet stalls along the Fuzimiao promenade and inside Laomendong; jars of the syrup sell year-round.

Where to shop

Fuzimiao Qinhuai Scenic Belt (夫子庙秦淮风光带)

The market quarter on the inner Qinhuai River, organized around a Confucius temple first founded in 1034 and rebuilt many times, most recently as a Ming-Qing-style block from 1984. A riverside boardwalk links the temple court, paddle-boat docks, snack halls and craft stalls; lanterns run evenings along the water.

Where: South of Jiankang Road, Qinhuai District; Metro Line 3 Fuzimiao stop.

Laomendong Historic Block (老门东)

A restored lane district at the foot of the 600-year Ming city wall east of Zhonghua Gate, reopened in 2013 after a block-wide rebuild. Three principal alleys pack sweet stalls, bookshops and craft studios into Qing-and-Republican-style facades; a small gate square hosts weekend markets and lanes stay busy past 21:00 in season.

Where: Inside Zhonghuamen, Qinhuai District, two metro stops south of Xinjiekou.

Xinjiekou (新街口)

The roundabout marketed as China's densest department-store cluster: more than 20 malls and retail centers sit within one kilometer of the central Sun Yat-sen statue, including the Central Emporium, trading since 1936. Metro Lines 1 and 2 cross directly beneath, and underground passages link all four corners, which keeps the hub usable in rain.

Where: The centerpoint where Gulou, Xuanwu, Qinhuai and Jianye districts meet; Xinjiekou interchange.

Shiziqiao Food Lane (狮子桥美食街)

A pedestrian snack lane off Hunan Road, developed into a food street in the 1990s: pasty, duck-blood and dessert brands plus sit-down local restaurants in two short blocks. Lunch and weekend evenings draw the heaviest queues; branch shops of old Nanjing names make it the working backup when Fuzimiao overflows.

Where: Hunan Road, Gulou District, near the Xuanwumen end; Metro Line 1 Xuanwumen stop.

Eating and shopping in Nanjing: field notes

Salted duck sells whole and half; counters at Xinjiekou department stores and both airports vacuum-pack birds that travel well. Soup dumplings and pasties peak at breakfast and lunch — Jiming Tangbao queues shorten after 13:00 — while Fuzimiao stalls run long evenings. Duck-blood counters restock twice a day, so mid-service bowls beat opening-rush ones.

Pair the two poles: Xinjiekou for malls and the 1936 Central Emporium, Laomendong for lane-scale crafts behind the 600-year wall. Fuzimiao turns busiest on weekend evenings once the riverside lanterns switch on; weekday mornings suit photographs and buying sweets without lines. Shiziqiao splits the difference at lunch.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the one dish Nanjing is known for?

Salted duck — brine-cured records reach the Six Dynasties, the craft standardized under the Ming, and autumn birds earn the osmanthus-duck label. Duck-blood vermicelli soup and Qijiawan beef pasties form the modern street counterweight, both tied to neighborhoods south and west of the old center.

Fuzimiao or Laomendong for an evening out?

Fuzimiao is the larger, older riverside quarter around the temple founded in 1034, heavier on dessert stalls and boat traffic. Laomendong, reopened in 2013 inside the wall lanes, keeps smaller shops and a slower pace. They sit within a few metro stops of each other, so one evening covers one end and a second visit the other.

Can I carry salted duck home as a gift?

Yes — counters at the 1936 Central Emporium, Xinjiekou malls and airport shops sell vacuum-packed whole or half ducks with a sealed shelf life of weeks when chilled. Autumn stock, labeled osmanthus duck, is the traditional benchmark; buy from deli counters rather than open stalls for proper packaging.

How do you eat Nanjing soup dumplings without scalding?

Nip the skin, drink the broth, then eat: aspic in the filling melts during a 10-minute steam, so every piece holds hot liquid. Locals add black vinegar with ginger shreds and start with a small basket of 6–8; a second basket arrives within minutes if needed.

Where do locals actually eat pidu noodles?

At old-town counters between 11:30 and 13:00, especially in Mingwalang alley near Xinjiekou, where a small bowl still stacks six to eight toppings including fried pork skin. Shops run short of pidu by mid-afternoon, so treat it as a lunch dish rather than a dinner one.
