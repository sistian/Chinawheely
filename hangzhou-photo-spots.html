West Lake (西湖) is the anchor of most itineraries here.

Overview: Photography in Hangzhou

West Lake is the most-photographed water in China, which is a problem rather than an opportunity, and the way through it is to stop photographing the lake and start photographing the details. The causeway willows, the stone pagodas on the small islands, the cliff carvings at Feilai Peak, the persimmon trees in the Xixi reed beds and the terraced tea slopes above Meijiawu all give you subjects that are geographically unique and far less crowded than the classic view.

Bai Causeway and the Broken Bridge (白堤断桥)

The Bai Causeway is the white stone causeway running south from the Broken Bridge to the Solitary Hill, and it was built on the same dredged-mud principle as the Su Causeway but later, in the Ming. The Broken Bridge at its north end is the classic West Lake subject, and the reason to go at 6 am rather than midday is the light on the snow-white balustrade and the Baochu Pagoda across the water. Sunrise gives you the eastern sky behind the causeway with the pagoda silhouetted, and the still water carries a reflection that is gone by 9 am when the wind picks up.

best light: sunrise · landmark

Getting there: Park at the Huanglongdongkou or Lingyin Road car parks and walk, or take the electric shuttle bus around the lake. The Broken Bridge is a short walk from the Wulinmen area on the east side.

  • The composition that works is the causeway leading diagonally in from the lower left with Baochu Pagoda on the opposite bank.
  • The white balustrade holds a warm tint at sunrise, which separates it from the grey of the water.
  • A long lens from the water's edge at the south end compresses the causeway and the pagoda together.

Three Pools Mirroring the Moon (三潭印月)

The three stone pagodas on the small islet in the northern lake are the motif of the West Lake's most famous seal, the one that appears on the 1979 foreign exchange note and on the reverse of the fifth series 1 yuan banknote of 2004. Each pagoda has five round holes and the original design put a coin in each hole with a candle underneath, so the light would read as three moons mirrored on the water. The pagodas were built in 1089 by the prefect Su Shi alongside the causeway, and the islet was shaped at the same time, so the whole composition is one piece of 11th century engineering.

best light: golden hour · landmark

Getting there: Reached by boat from the Huagang and Yue Temple docks, or by walking the Bai Causeway and the short causeway from the Solitary Hill side. The island is about 500 m across with a covered walkway, a pagoda and a small bridge.

  • Shoot from the low stone edge on the island's north side, where the pagodas sit directly against the water.
  • Evening, when the lights on the island are on and the sky is still bright, gives the strongest contrast.
  • On a windy day the reflection is impossible, so check the lake surface before committing.

Feilai Peak Carvings (飞来峰造像)

Feilai Peak is a 168 m limestone outcrop above Lingyin Temple carrying some 470 Buddhist carvings running from the Eastern Jin through to the Song, and they are the densest concentration of ancient Buddhist stone sculpture in the south of China. The reason to photograph them rather than the temple is that they are relief carved directly into the rock face, so they change dramatically with the angle of light. The Tang-period laughing Buddha, a plump figure with an enormous exposed belly, is the most reproduced, and the Song and Yuan niches above the cave are much subtler. The cliff is in permanent shade under an overhang, which makes it a genuine lighting problem but also protects the stone.

best light: all day · landmark

Getting there: A 200-step climb above the Lingyin Temple complex, on the same ticket. The path continues along the ridge for about 1 km past the cave to the Laughing Buddha.

  • A torch or a small LED panel bounced into the niche is the only way to light the carvings properly.
  • Look for the Song and Yuan figures higher up the cliff, which most visitors walk straight past.
  • Rainy and overcast conditions give the most even light on the relief and also bring out the moss in the niches.

The Xixi Wetland Reed Boardwalks (西溪湿地)

The Xixi wetland covers 11.5 square kilometres with about 90 percent of it water, plum, willow, persimmon and camphor, and it is managed on a deliberately low-intervention model, which means plank paths, mud flats and hand-pulled rowing boats instead of a big steel boardwalk. The photographic subject is the persimmon grove along the Fu Embankment, where the trees are trained low and wide over the water, and in October they carry bright orange fruit against bare branches with the reeds behind. Late afternoon gives a warm backlight through the reed beds and a long reflection in the channels. The observation tower at the north end gives the overview of the pond pattern.

best light: sunset · nature

Getting there: Hezhu Street gate has the main car park and the electric boat landing. The inner paths are a 30-minute walk from the gate, or a rowing boat from the landing takes you straight into the reed beds.

  • The hand-pulled rowing boat takes a passenger on a slow circuit of the inner water, which is the only way to reach the best persimmon groves.
  • A polariser is essential on the water, because the surface glare otherwise removes the reflections entirely.
  • October brings both the persimmon colour and a much lower visitor count than the spring blossom.

Longjing Tea Terraces (龙井茶园)

The tea bushes on the slopes above Meijiawu and Longjing village are planted in rows that follow the contour lines of the hill, and the resulting pattern of parallel green bands curving across the slope is a completely different kind of landscape from anything else in Hangzhou. In spring the picking season covers the slopes with small blue-grey tents and dozens of pickers spread along the rows, and in autumn the low sun turns the ridges into alternating bands of light and shadow. The China National Tea Museum at Shuangfeng is at the bottom of the run and provides the context for what you are looking at.

best light: golden hour · nature

Getting there: Drive up the Longjing Road from the west end of the lake to Meijiawu, where the terraces begin. Village tea houses sit at the roadside and provide the elevated viewpoint.

  • The best view is from the tea house terrace above Meijiawu, looking down the slope across the rows to the valley beyond.
  • A longer lens compresses the contour lines into an abstract pattern of repeated curves.
  • Avoid the mid-morning window, when the haze off the city softens everything into grey.

Leifeng Pagoda at Sunset (雷峰塔)

The present Leifeng Pagoda was completed in 2002 on the site of a much older tower, and it was deliberately built as a steel-frame structure clad in copper so that it would read as a wooden Tang-style pavilion rather than a modern building. It stands on the south-west shore of the lake directly opposite Baochu Pagoda, and the pair is the lake's classic two-pagoda composition. The ruined original, which collapsed in 1924 after years of illegal digging at its base, gave its name to Leifeng Sunset, one of the West Lake's Ten Scenes and one of the most painted views in Chinese art. The new pagoda's interior is hollow with a glass floor over the foundations.

best light: sunset · golden-hour

Getting there: Metro Line 2 to Chunanmen and walk, or drive to the Leifeng Pagoda car park. The pagoda is on the south-west shore, opposite the Su Causeway's first bridge.

  • Shoot from the lakeside path north of the pagoda, with Baochu Pagoda across the water in the same frame.
  • The copper-clad exterior takes the sunset colour best in the 20 minutes before the sun drops behind the hills.
  • The old pagoda's foundation pit is preserved inside the new building under the glass floor, which is worth a frame.

Hefang Street (河坊街)

Hefang Street is the reconstructed Song dynasty commercial street on the southern side of the West Lake, running 1,180 m from the lakeshore up towards the south gate of the imperial city, and it is lined with a mix of traditional-style shopfronts, old workshops and lantern-strung paving. The photographic interest is the collision of eras, since the street sits directly against the 1912 Republic-era arcades and the modern shopping centres at its western end. At night the lanterns are strung in lines down the street and the paving reflects them, and the whole thing sits against the dark mass of the hill.

best light: blue hour · night

Getting there: Metro Line 7 to Hefang Street station, with the street running uphill from the lake. Parking is available in the side streets and in the Yan'an Road garage.

  • Shoot up the street from the lakeside end, where the lantern lines recede towards the Drum Tower.
  • The wet paving after rain gives a much stronger reflection than a dry street.
  • The upper end of the street near the Qinghefang historic district is calmer and has better period detail.

Working around a lake that everyone has photographed

The technical challenge at West Lake is not the light, which is soft and forgiving, but the impossibility of finding a version of the classic view that is not already in ten thousand photographs. The way through is a telephoto and a detail, not a wide angle and a panorama. The causeway balustrades, the stone pagodas on the small islands, the cliff carvings at Feilai Peak and the terrace lines at Meijiawu are all subjects that reward a 70 to 200 mm lens and a low viewpoint.

Reflection is the second theme worth technical effort. A polariser cuts the surface glare on the lake and the Xixi channels, but on West Lake you also want a low angle from the water's edge with the sun behind you. A graduated or bracketed exposure keeps the sky from blowing out while holding the darker water and the tree line, which is what makes the classic pagoda-and-reflection frames work.

The third thing worth planning is the time of day for crowds rather than light. Before 8 am the lakeside is empty enough to shoot the Broken Bridge unobstructed; between 10 am and 4 pm it is at its worst; and from about half an hour before sunset the light is better but the crowds return. Most of the best Hangzhou frames come from the hour before sunrise and the hour after the crowds have thinned in the late afternoon.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I get a West Lake shot that does not look like every other one?

Stop shooting the lake and shoot the details: a telephoto on the three stone pagodas of the small islet, the white balustrade of the Bai Causeway at sunrise, the carved cliff reliefs at Feilai Peak, or the contour rows of the tea terraces above Meijiawu. A 70 to 200 mm lens from a low position at the water's edge gives you a compressed, graphic frame that is impossible with the standard wide establishing shot.

What time of day is best at West Lake?

Before 8 am for light and emptiness together, and again in the last hour of daylight for the pagoda compositions. The lake is 6.39 square kilometres with a 15 km shoreline, and between roughly 10 am and 4 pm the tour groups fill the causeways. Sunrise at the Broken Bridge also gives the flattest water, which is what makes the reflections work, and the wind usually picks up later in the day.

Can I photograph the cliff carvings at Feilai Peak?

Yes, but they need artificial help. The 470 carvings at Feilai Peak are relief cut directly into the rock under a permanent overhang, so the natural light is flat and dim and a torch or a small bounce panel is needed to bring out the depth. The Tang laughing Buddha is the famous one, but the Song and Yuan niches higher along the ridge are more interesting and almost nobody goes further than the cave. Overcast and rainy weather actually gives the most even light on the stone.

Is Xixi Wetland worth a half day, and how do I get into the good parts?

It is worth it, and the access method matters. The site is 11.5 square kilometres, about 90 percent of it water and wetland, and the best sections are the inner reed beds and the persimmon grove along the Fu Embankment, which are a long walk from the Hezhu Street gate. The hand-pulled rowing boats from the landing reach the inner water directly. October gives the persimmon colour with low crowds.

How do I avoid reflections of the crowd in the lake at Hefang Street?

Shoot the street itself rather than the lake, and use the wet paving as your surface. The upper part of Hefang Street, near the Qinghefang historic district and the Drum Tower, has better period architecture and far fewer people than the lakeside end. Rain or recent rain gives the strongest lantern reflections, and a polariser controls the glare on the paving without losing the colour.

Are drone flights allowed over West Lake?

No. The entire West Lake cultural landscape was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2011, and the lakeside area is under tight control with restricted airspace for the surrounding city. The raised viewpoints that work without a drone are the Yuhuang Pavilion terrace at the top of the hill above the south lake, the Fuzhi Pagoda terrace, and the upper levels of the Leifeng Pagoda, which give you more height over the causeways than most drone shots would have achieved anyway.
