Overview: Tea Culture in Fuzhou

Fuzhou is the home of scented jasmine tea: green tea layered with freshly picked jasmine over several nights, a technique the city has run at commercial scale since the 19th century. The grade ladder climbs from 3 scenting rounds to 9 for the finest lots, and the craft is woven through the old lane quarter of Sanfang Qixiang, where covered bowls of it are poured all day. The city also trades on the black tea made 300 km north in the Wuyi mountains, and on the oolong grown along the southern Fujian coast. Three names do the daily work of the trade here, each brewed in a different pot.

The tea

Jasmine tea (茉莉花茶)

Jasmine tea is scented rather than fermented: green tea is layered with fresh jasmine blossoms over several nights in Fuzhou, so the scent is absorbed rather than added. The flowers are picked in the evening, when they are at their strongest. Standard grades take 3 scenting rounds, top lots up to 9, and the spent blossoms are then removed - the best packs show leaf only.

How to have it here: In Fuzhou it is an everyday pot rather than a special occasion: covered bowls in the lanes of Sanfang Qixiang, corner shops and breakfast tables. Labels state the scenting count, from 3 rounds upward. The base tea is spring-harvested, then scented in July and August when the jasmine crop peaks, so a finished lot carries two dates in effect - a picking date for the leaf and a month for the flower. Blossoms are picked in the evening and layered onto the leaf the same night, which is why the finished product keeps a scent that synthetic flavouring does not reproduce.

Lapsang Souchong (正山小种)

Lapsang Souchong comes from Tongmu Village in the Wuyi mountains of northern Fujian, about 300 km from Fuzhou. The leaves are withered over pine fires, which gives the black tea its smoke character - the original 'black tea' of the China trade, recorded at Tongmu from the 17th century. It is sold across Fuzhou's tea shops alongside the local jasmine.

How to have it here: Brew it at 90-95°C for two to three minutes; the smoke stands up to milk in the Hong Kong manner. Most drinkers in Fuzhou treat it as the regional black-tea option beside jasmine, and the producing village of Tongmu is reachable by a roughly 300 km trip north to Wuyishan if you want the workshops themselves. Look on the pack for the Tongmu name rather than a generic Wuyi label, since the smoke character comes from the pine fires of that one valley and is the mark the tea is sold on.

Tieguanyin (铁观音)

Tieguanyin is a semi-fermented oolong from Anxi County in southern Fujian. The leaves are rolled into dark, tightly packed pellets, and the same pot can be brewed 8 or 10 times, with each round sweeter than the last. Anxi sits in the province's southern tea country, well away from Fuzhou, but the tea is a standard item on Fuzhou shop counters and in the tea rooms attached to restaurants.

How to have it here: It is served gongfu-style rather than in a covered bowl: a small clay pot, 110 ml, about 8 grams of leaf, and pours of 15 ml or less taken in a run of eight to ten rounds. The rolled pellets are dropped whole and never crushed, and each round is timed from the moment the pot is filled, usually 10 seconds for the first and a few seconds longer after that. Because the leaf withstands repeated steeping, a single pot is expected to cover a whole table rather than one cup.

Jasmine tea etiquette in Fuzhou

The vessel is the gaiwan, a covered bowl of about 150 ml holding roughly 5 grams of leaf. The lid does double duty: held slightly ajar to pour, then lifted to the nose and inhaled before the first sip, because jasmine grades are judged on the scent first. Water around 85-90°C, first steep about 45 seconds, three refills as a rule.

Fuzhou pours for the table, not for the individual: the host fills guests' cups first, in age order, and keeps the pot moving. A guest taps 2 fingers when topped up. Filling a cup to the brim is poor form - 7 or 8 tenths full is the local standard - and asking how many scenting rounds a lot took, 3 or 9, is the ordinary way to talk quality.

The covered bowl is set down rather than held while talking, and the person pouring keeps the spout clear of the cup rim so no drips land on the saucer. Poured leaves are never fished out with a spoon at a Fuzhou table; spent leaf is tipped out after 3 or 4 rounds, and a second brewing is offered only if the host asks. Anything scented is treated as a group property, and the label - base tea, scenting count, harvest year - is discussed before the first cup is taken.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does the scenting count on the label mean?

It is the number of times the base green tea was layered with fresh jasmine blossoms - from 3 rounds in standard grades up to 9 in the finest. More rounds mean more flowers and more labour, so the price scales with the count. A 7- or 9-round lot needs several nights of scenting in August, when the jasmine crop peaks, and each round is followed by a drying stage before the next layer goes on. Top lots end with the spent blossoms removed entirely, leaving leaf only in the pack.

Why is Fuzhou associated with jasmine tea?

The city has run commercial scenting since the 19th century, sitting between jasmine-growing fields on the plain and green-tea producers in the hills. The July-August blossom season, the several-night layering method and the evening picking of the flowers all point back to Fuzhou workshops, and Sanfang Qixiang teahouses still pour it as the local default.

Is Lapsang Souchong also from Fuzhou?

No. It is a black tea from Tongmu Village in the Wuyi mountains of northern Fujian, about 300 km from Fuzhou, withered over pine fires since the 17th century. It is stocked in Fuzhou as part of the wider Fujian tea trade, but its origin is the mountain country, not the capital of the province's scenting craft.

Where can I drink it in Fuzhou?

Teahouses in the Sanfang Qixiang lane quarter serve it in covered bowls, the standard 150 ml vessel with about 5 grams of leaf. Tea shops across the city grade by the scenting count printed on the pack, and tasting two or three counts side by side before buying is a normal request, not a special favour.

How is Tieguanyin different from the local jasmine?

Jasmine tea is scented green tea with the fragrance of flowers added over several nights, and it is judged on scent. Tieguanyin is a semi-fermented oolong from Anxi County in southern Fujian, judged on body and infusion length, and one pot of its pellets can be brewed eight or ten times. The two are served in different vessels, a 150 ml covered bowl against a 110 ml clay pot, and they take different water temperatures: 85-90°C against the near-boiling water used for the rolled oolong pellets.

What water temperature and timing should I use in Fuzhou?

For jasmine tea, water at about 85-90°C and a first steep of roughly 45 seconds, with three refills after it. For Lapsang Souchong, 90-95°C for two to three minutes. Gongfu-style oolong takes far less water per pour, about 15 ml, and 8 grams of leaf in a 110 ml pot. Judges at Fuzhou tea competitions score aroma and infusion colour separately, so both numbers end up on the scorecard.
