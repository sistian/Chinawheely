Why transfer to the Xi'an City Wall?

It is one of the oldest and best-preserved city walls in China, exhibiting the complete defensive apparatus — moat, drawbridges, watch towers, corner towers, parapet walls and gate towers — on a single circuit. In March 1961 it was designated among China's first batch of nationally protected sites, and since 2008 it has sat on the country's Tentative World Heritage List under "City Walls of the Ming and Qing Dynasties", nominated under criteria iii and iv.

The wall keeps its original rectangular plan with almost every stretch restored or rebuilt at some point in its history. The moat that surrounds it is 18 metres wide and 6 metres deep, and the gate towers punctuate the circuit at the cardinal points. Zhu Yuanzhang, first emperor of the Ming dynasty (1368–1644), ordered the fortification to protect the northwestern capital, founded at Xi'an in 1370, against the Northern Yuan and internal unrest.

  • Perimeter of 13.74 kilometres on a rectangular Ming-era plan
  • Wall height 12 metres; top width 12–14 metres on a base over 15 metres thick
  • Moat 18 metres wide and 6 metres deep, with drawbridges at the gates
  • Among China's first batch of nationally protected sites, designated March 1961
  • On China's Tentative World Heritage List since 2008

What to do at the Xi'an City Wall once you arrive

The full circuit on foot is a commitment: the walkway along the top of the wall typically takes four hours to cover end to end. Most visitors enter at the South Gate, the most complete of the gate complexes with its archery tower and moat bridge, and walk or cycle a section rather than the whole 13.74 kilometres. The parapets on both sides mark the original fighting positions, and the corner towers at the rectangle's corners give the clearest view down two faces at once.

Below the wall, the moat and the gates structure the old city's street grid; the Bell Tower and Drum Tower sit inside the circuit, and the Muslim Quarter begins a short walk from the North Gate area. Bicycles can be hired for the top walkway at the main gates — the 12-to-14-metre width was made for it — and an evening circuit passes the gates lit along the ramparts, a marked contrast to the daytime view of the moat and towers in plain stone.

  • Walking the entire 13.74-kilometre walkway takes about four hours; plan a partial circuit otherwise
  • Enter at the South Gate for the most complete gate complex
  • Bike hire operates at the main gates for the full-width walkway
  • The Bell Tower, Drum Tower and Muslim Quarter all lie inside the wall within walking distance

Arriving at Xi'an Xianyang International Airport

Xianyang covers a 12-square-kilometre site with terminals T1, T2, T3 and T5 in operation, and ranks as the nation's 11th-busiest airport by passenger traffic and 16th-busiest for cargo. China Eastern maintains a hub at the airport, joined by Sichuan Airlines and Hainan Airlines, and Phase 1 construction began in August 1987 on the site of Xianyang's former airfield. The airport lies 25 kilometres northwest of the city it serves, inside Xianyang's administrative boundary.

Public transport into the city is slower than in coastal hubs, and the wall's gates sit deep in the old street grid, where the last kilometre matters. A booked car with flight tracking takes the expressway from the airport to the South Gate area in one seat, which is the straightforward option after a late arrival; the metro network reaches the walled city's edge for travellers travelling light, with the Bell Tower stations inside the circuit.

What the transfer costs

The 25-kilometre run from Xianyang to the city centre sits in a moderate fare band for a Chinese airport transfer; metered taxis quote on the meter plus the expressway toll, and private sedan transfers are typically offered in the 150 to 250 yuan range for the single-seat run to the wall's gates, driver waiting and toll included in the quoted price.

Families and small groups commonly move up to the van class rather than split into two cars, and all four gates have drop-off space on the moat-side roads. Against the meter, the fixed quote holds if your flight lands into the evening banks; the meter does not care that you waited forty minutes at baggage claim, and the queue at Xianyang's ranks lengthens sharply when the hub banks arrive together.

Ask the driver for the South Gate approach across the moat bridge rather than the ring roads outside the wall; the gate squares have short-stay drop zones, while the wall-side ring road itself has no stopping places.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long is the Xi'an City Wall?

The circuit runs 13.74 kilometres — 8.54 miles — on a rectangular plan around the old city. The wall stands 12 metres high, measures 12 to 14 metres across the top, and rests on a base more than 15 metres thick. Walking the full walkway typically takes four hours.

When was the Xi'an City Wall built?

Construction began around 1370 under the Hongwu Emperor, first ruler of the Ming dynasty (1368–1644), when Xi'an was established as the capital of northwestern Shaanxi. The builders incorporated older fortified embankments from the Sui and Tang dynasties into the new circuit, so parts of the foundations are centuries older than the Ming wall itself.

How far is Xianyang Airport from the City Wall?

The airport stands 25 kilometres northwest of central Xi'an, within Xianyang city's administrative area. The expressway run to the walled city takes roughly 40 to 50 minutes in normal traffic and longer at peak, ending at one of the gate squares on the moat's inner side.

Can you cycle on top of the wall?

Yes. Hire points at the main gates rent bicycles for the top walkway, whose 12-to-14-metre width carries riders comfortably alongside pedestrians. The full 13.74-kilometre loop is a realistic ride, whereas on foot the same circuit typically takes four hours end to end.

What protection status does the wall have?

In March 1961 it was listed among China's first batch of nationally protected cultural sites. Since 2008 it has also appeared on China's Tentative List for UNESCO inscription as part of the "City Walls of the Ming and Qing Dynasties" serial nomination, under criteria iii and iv.
