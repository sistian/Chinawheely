Overview: Eating and Shopping in Tianjin

Tianjin eats in the morning at a street stall and in the afternoon behind a shop counter that has been on the same street since the 1920s. The city on the Hai river has three snacks that every local can name without thinking: steamed buns from Goubuli, a folded mung bean pancake called jianbing guozi, and a twisted fried dough from Shibajie Street. Each has a written history and, in two cases, a non-heritage listing. What changed in the last twenty years is the shopping: the concession-era villas of the Five Great Avenues and the Italian quarter are now the parts visitors remember.

What to eat

Goubuli Steamed Buns (狗不理包子)

Founded in 1858 by Gao Guiyou, a cook from Xiazhu village in Wuqing county who worked for three years at the Liu family's steam kitchen on the south bank of the Grand Canal before opening his own shop, Dejuhao. The nickname explains the name: Gouzi sold buns and did not bother talking to customers. The buns are steamed in tiered wooden baskets and are known for a thin, tender skin folded into eighteen pleats over a filling of pork and cabbage or beef, with a hot clear soup poured in at the table.

Where: The shop moved into Tianxiang market in the former French concession in the 1920s, in the area of today's Quanyechang, and became state-owned on 15 March 1956 at its current address on Shandong Road, the former site of the Fengzeyuan restaurant. Private dining rooms were added in 1988, the company was reorganised as a group in 1992, and Tongrentang acquired it in 2005 for 106 million yuan.

Jianbing Guozi (煎饼果子)

A mung bean batter spread thin on a griddle, given an egg, spread with sweet bean paste, fermented bean curd and chilli, then rolled around a piece of fried dough called a guo. It is rolled rather than folded, eaten standing up, and finished with a scattering of sesame. The dough inside is the point: the crisp fried piece keeps its texture inside the soft pancake. The first written record appears on 20 November 1933 in the supplement of the Tianjin edition of Ta Kung Pao, where it was still called a guobo.

Where: Street stalls across the city, especially around the morning markets of Nankai, Hongqiao and Dongma Road, and the breakfast queues outside Goubuli. It was listed as a fourth-batch municipal intangible cultural heritage item of Tianjin in June 2017.

Shibajie Mahua (十八街麻花)

A braided deep-fried dough twist, made by folding dough into a rope, knotting it, and frying it so that the layers separate. One strand is worked through with a filling of preserved plum or osmanthus crisp, which is why the twist has a sweet core inside a plain shell. It keeps for months without going stale, which made it a standard gift rather than a snack to be eaten on the spot. It took shape at the beginning of the 1920s when the owner of a failing shop on Nanlou took over the counter and worked with his two assistants.

Where: The counter was on Shibajie Street, and the shop took the name Guifaxiang from the two men, Fan Guicai and Fan Guilin, who ran it. It was first shown at the Canton Fair in 1971, named a Tianjin municipal quality product in 1981, and became a company with branches in Shijiazhuang, Taiyuan and Zhengzhou in 1983.

Erdoyan Fried Cakes (耳朵眼炸糕)

A glutinous rice cake filled with sweetened red bean paste, formed with a rice crust and fried until the outside is crisp and the bean paste inside still hot enough to burn the roof of the mouth. It is eaten in one or two bites, standing up, and it is at its best in the first minutes after it leaves the oil. The name is the address: the shop opened at the corner of Erduoyan Hutong on the street outside the north gate in 1892.

Where: Founded as Liu Ji Zha Gao Pu by Liu Wanchun in the eighteenth year of the Guangxu reign, and later renamed Zengshengcheng, though everyone called it by the alley. Counters for it still stand around Ancient Culture Street and at the entrance to the city from the north.

Bengdou Zhang (崩豆张)

A crisp soybean snack in the Tianjin manner, made from soaked soybeans that are fried until the skins blister and split away from the beans, which then crumble into a flaky texture. The classic products are hu pi zheng xiang beng dou with a crisp outer skin, and a darker black-skinned version roasted longer for a deeper flavour. The beans are eaten as a snack with tea rather than as a dish at the table.

Where: Founded at the end of the Jiaqing reign by Zhang Decai, a cook in the imperial kitchen, and named by his three sons, Zhang Yongtai and his brothers, who settled in Tianjin and opened the main Bengdou Zhang shop.

Guoren Zhang (果仁张)

Nuts and seeds in the Tianjin style, most famously amber peanuts and amber walnuts, where the sugar is cooked to a clear film that hardens around the kernel and leaves it glassy and brittle, which is the hanging-frost series the business is known for. Other lines include tiger-skin peanuts, plain fragrant peanuts, milk-flavoured melon seeds and five-spice pine nuts, with modern additions in cocoa, coffee, curry, mint and sea-weed flavours.

Where: Founded in 1830 by the Zhang family, whose first two generations cooked in the imperial kitchen and whose third-generation member Zhang Huishan prepared food for state banquets. The fourth generation, Zhang Yifeng and his wife Chen Jing, developed the shop and created the hanging-frost series.

Where to shop

Ancient Culture Street (古文化街)

A rebuilt pedestrian street of about 700 metres running north to south on the east bank of the Hai river, built in the late 1980s in a deliberately old-fashioned style: tiled roofs, painted eaves and shopfronts in the shape of a Qing dynasty trading street. The entrance arch is cast in copper and carries four characters naming Tianjin as its old home. It functions as the city's folk culture and tourism commercial centre, with craft workshops, paper-cut studios, clay figure studios and snack counters.

Where: North of the Ancient Culture Street Bridge on the east side of the Hai river, in the old Nankai and Dongcheng area near the river ferries.

Five Great Avenues (五大道)

The name given to the former British concession extension outside the Waizi river, laid out in 1903 and built up on landfill from the dredging of the Hai river between 1919 and 1926. By the 1920s the plots carried large villas, and the six east-west roads now called Machang Road, Munan Road, Dali Road, Changde Road, Chongqing Road and Chengdu Road were once named after Majiacao, Hong Kong, Singapore, Colombo, Cambridge and Edinburgh. The styles are deliberately mixed: Italian Baroque, French, British, Japanese and Russian. In 2011 the district was listed as a historic district, in 2013 thirty-nine of its buildings were named national key cultural relics, and in 2015 it became one of the first national historical and cultural streets.

Where: South of the centre in Nanshi district, around Minyuan Stadium and the Munan Park along the Machang Road bend.

Italian Quarter (意式风情区)

Built on the former Italian concession, the only Italian concession China ever had and the only large Italian-style building group in Asia. Its boundary was signed on 7 June 1902 between Tang Shaoyi, the Tianjin customs commissioner, and the Italian minister in China, covering 771 acres between the Austrian and Russian concessions, with the Jinshan railway to the north and the Hai river to the south. The first consul surveyed the site, used spoil from dredging the river to fill the marshland, laid drainage, and built garden housing with a club, a market, a police station and an Italian garden. The concession was taken back in 1946 and the street was redeveloped as a heritage project from 2002, opening to visitors in 2008.

Where: Hebei district, along Freedom Road and Minzu Road, on the north bank of the Hai river opposite the eye of the river.

Quanyechang (劝业场)

One of the comprehensive commercial buildings of the first half of the twentieth century in China's large cities, in the same family as the Beijing, Qingdao, Chengdu and Wuhan buildings, and the best known of them. The block around it was the Tianxiang market, the concession-era trading ground to which Goubuli moved in the 1920s, and the surrounding streets still carry department stores, jewellers and old food counters.

Where: Nanshi district, on the streets around Nankai Road and Quanyechang street, two blocks north of the Five Great Avenues.

Eating and shopping in Tianjin

The three famous snacks are eaten at different hours. Jianbing guozi is a breakfast bought at a folding table on the pavement, and it is finished before you have walked a block. Goubuli is a sit-down business of long standing, and the shop on Shandong Road serves the same eighteen-pleat buns it has served since 1956. Shibajie mahua and the fried cakes are bought to carry, and the mahua in particular is the one to buy in several boxes at once, since it keeps for months.

For shopping, the practical split is between the souvenir streets and the residential ones. Ancient Culture Street and the Italian Quarter are built for visitors and open for most of the day; the Five Great Avenues are a living neighbourhood whose villas are only visible from the pavement, so walking the six roads rather than the tourist entrances is what makes the difference. Quanyechang is the working shopping district, and the stalls around it are the ones that still do what they did in the 1920s.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which Tianjin snack is worth queueing for?

It depends on what you want. Goubuli is the historic name, founded in 1858 and now state-owned on Shandong Road, and it is the one with the thickest history. Jianbing guozi has the shortest written record, first printed on 20 November 1933, and it is the one that still gets eaten standing on a pavement. Shibajie mahua is the one to buy as a gift, since the twist keeps for months. Eat all three; they cost very little.

Is jianbing guozi a pancake or a wrap?

A wrap, and the filling is what makes it distinctive. The batter is mung bean, spread thin on a griddle, then given an egg, sweet bean paste, fermented bean curd and chilli, and rolled around a piece of fried dough with sesame on the outside. It is sold in a paper sleeve and eaten immediately, usually on the street, because the pancake softens within minutes.

How much of the concession-era architecture is still standing?

The Italian quarter keeps one of the largest and most complete surviving Italian-style building groups in China, laid out between 1902 and 1946 over 771 acres. The Five Great Avenues were built on drained and filled river land from 1903 and developed through the 1920s, and thirty-nine of its buildings are now listed as national key cultural relics. Both areas are ordinary city districts rather than museum sites, which is why the buildings are best appreciated from the street.

What is Ancient Culture Street for?

It is a rebuilt 700-metre pedestrian street on the east bank of the Hai river, put up in a deliberate Qing dynasty commercial style with a copper arch at the entrance reading Tianjin as its old home. It concentrates the city's craft and snack trades, including paper cutting, clay figures and New Year print workshops, and it is the easiest single place to see what Tianjin makes rather than what it eats.

Can I buy tea and nuts as souvenirs?

Yes, and they are the local answer to the same question in other cities. Guoren Zhang has been making nuts in Tianjin since 1830, and its amber peanuts and hanging-frost walnuts are the best known of them. Bengdou Zhang, founded at the end of the Jiaqing reign by a cook from the imperial kitchen, does the same with fried soybeans. Both are sold by weight at counters rather than in gift boxes, so buy them late in the day if you are travelling onward.
