Traditional Tea Houses in Suzhou

Suzhou is a city where tea is drunk through the day rather than in a single sitting. The tea houses and tea counters below are the places worth stopping at, and a car with a waiting driver is what makes it practical to visit more than one. The tea counters in Suzhou sit on the commercial streets, so a tea stop and a sightseeing stop are usually the same stop.

Tea houses around the old quarter

Most of Suzhou's tea trade happens in the streets around the old quarter, where the tea shops pour a pot for you at the counter rather than selling by the tin. Ask for a longjing, a shou pu-erh or a jasmine blend and they will let you taste before you buy.

Morning tea and the market stalls

Tea drinking in Suzhou starts early: the markets open with the first kettle of the day, and the stalls around the old commercial streets are where the city's own blend is put together.

Overview: Tea Culture in Suzhou

Biluochun grows on two peninsula hills in Lake Tai - the Dongting East and West hills, roughly 35 km southwest of Suzhou's old city. First known as 'scary fragrance', the tea was renamed by the Kangxi Emperor around 1700. Its picking standard is extreme: one bud with one unfolded leaf, adding up to about 60,000 picks per 500 grams of finished tea.

The tea

Biluochun (碧螺春)

Biluochun is a tightly rolled green tea from Dongting Mountain on the shore of Lake Tai near Suzhou. The whole leaf - bud and leaf together - is rolled into small downy spirals, which is where the name 'snail-shell spring tea' comes from. The gardens are intercropped with loquat, peach and tangerine trees, an arrangement several centuries old that shades the bushes and touches the leaf.

How to have it here: Shops sell by picking date: Mingqian, before Qingming around April 4, tops the scale. In Suzhou the tea is poured in the teahouses along Pingjiang Road and Shantang Street, usually beside Suzhou-style pastries. The leaf is fine enough that shops brew it in glass on purpose, so the spirals can be watched unfurling.

Drinking Biluochun the Suzhou way

The leaf is small and tender, so the water stays gentle: 70-80°C, about 3 grams in a 150-200 ml glass. Many Suzhou drinkers use the water-first method - fill the glass, then scatter the leaf on top, so the downy spirals sink slowly instead of being scalded. One to two minutes for the first round; two rounds total, rarely more.

Tea in Suzhou arrives with food: the teahouse table is set for pastries as much as for the pot, and hosts pour in small cups. Guests tap two fingers to acknowledge a refill; cups are topped up before they empty, and leaving a little liquor in the cup at the end signals you are finished, not that the tea failed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is it called 'snail shell'?

The name Biluochun describes the shape: green, downy spirals rolled tight like small snail shells, picked in spring. The Kangxi Emperor is credited with fixing the name around 1700, replacing the older folk label 'scary fragrance'. One 500-gram lot of the top grade represents roughly 60,000 individual bud-and-leaf picks.

When is the harvest season?

Picking runs from late March to mid-April: the top Mingqian grade before Qingming around April 4-5, then Yuqian until Grain Rain around April 20. The Dongting hills sit about 35 km southwest of the old city, and April puts the intercropped fruit trees in blossom at the same time as the picking.

How does Biluochun differ from Longjing?

Shape first: Biluochun is curled and downy, Longjing flat and smooth. Brewing differs too - 70-80°C water for Biluochun against 80-85°C for Longjing, and the Suzhou tea is given to two rounds where the Hangzhou tea takes three. Both are graded by the same pre-Qingming and pre-Grain-Rain date ladder.

Where should I buy it in Suzhou?

Tea shops around Pingjiang Road and Shantang Street in the old city carry the full date range, and the pack should state the picking grade - Mingqian or Yuqian - plus a geographical-indication mark. Ask to see the leaf brewed in glass before paying; spirals that open unevenly usually mean a late-season or off-origin lot.
