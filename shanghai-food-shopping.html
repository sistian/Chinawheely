Overview: Eating and Shopping in Shanghai

Shanghai's food register runs from Nanxiang's soup-filled xiaolongbao, documented to the Qing dynasty, to 1930s pork-chop-and-rice-cake platters. This page lists five foods with dated origins and three shopping streets with measured lengths, so you can plan eating and buying around verifiable facts.

What to eat

Nanxiang xiaolongbao (南翔小笼包)

Small steamed buns with thin skin and hot pork broth inside, praised for small size, big filling, abundant juice and neat shape. Modern xiaolongbao took form in the Jiangnan region during the Daoguang reign, and the Shanghai school descends from the 'tight-ferment large meat bun' created by Huang Mingxian at the Rihuaxuan pastry shop in Nanxiang town, Jiading.

Where: The Yu Garden bazaar near the City God Temple keeps a famous Nanxiang branch; the dish is also standard at breakfast counters across the old Huangpu districts.

Shengjian mantou (生煎馒头)

Pan-fried pork buns with a pale top, thin crisp skin and a toasted base, scattered with sesame and scallion. The snack emerged in Suzhou early in the 20th century, entered Shanghai in the 1920s beside tea houses and hot-water stalls, and gained dedicated shops from the 1930s. Two schools exist: with pork-skin aspic in the filling, or leavened dough without it.

Where: Documented exponents include Wangjiasha, Dahuchun, Dongtaixiang and Xiaoyang Shengjian, with branches across the central districts; morning lines are routine.

Xiekehuang sesame pastry (蟹壳黄)

A small round baked flatbread of the Jiangsu-Shanghai-Anhui belt, crisp and oily without being greasy, colored golden-orange like a cooked crab shell — hence the name. Four classic fillings are recorded: shepherd's-purse, scallion oil, white sugar, and bean paste; the Huangshan version stuffs it with dried mustard greens and diced pork.

Where: Sold at traditional pastry counters around the City God Temple and Shanghai Old Street on Fangbang Middle Road; also common at old-city breakfast shops.

Pork chop with rice cakes (排骨年糕)

A Shanghai snack that rose in the 1930s: a large pork chop served with small, thin rice cakes that are deep-fried and braised. One documented style coats the chop in flour, water-chestnut starch, five-spice powder and egg before frying to a golden crust; another braises Wuxi-Changzhou pork in soy, sugar, wine, scallion and ginger until dark red. Sweet-savory by design.

Where: The best-documented names are Xiandelai and the Xiaochangzhou counter of the former Shuguang restaurant; branches operate in Huangpu and Hongkou.

Chicken-and-duck blood soup (鸡鸭血汤)

A breakfast soup of cubed chicken and duck blood in clear broth, recorded in Shanghai as one of the traditional companions to shengjian mantou alongside curried beef soup and fried-tofu vermicelli soup. Served in small bowls so it can be finished while the buns are hot; it remains a fixture of old-city morning menus rather than a dinner dish.

Where: Paired with shengjian at breakfast shops in the old South Market and City God Temple quarters; availability thins out after mid-morning.

Where to shop

Nanjing Road pedestrian street (南京路步行街)

East-west shopping avenue laid out in 1851 among the first roads of the foreign settlement, running from the Bund at Zhongshan East No.1 Road to Xizang Middle Road and styled 'China's No.1 business street'. The car-free section carries flagship department stores and heritage food shops; store hours concentrate between 10:00 and 22:00.

Where: Huangpu District, from the Bund west to Xizang Middle Road; served by several Line 2 and Line 10 stations.

Shanghai Old Street (Fangbang Middle Road) (上海老街（方浜中路）)

Fangbang Middle Road, 1,469 meters long and 6.1 to 17.6 meters wide, was built in 1913 by filling the Fangbang creek and renamed in 1946. The stretch in front of the Yu Garden and City God Temple is known as Shanghai Old Street, lined with snack stalls, pastry shops and tourist-goods stores through daytime hours.

Where: Huangpu District, immediately east of Yu Garden; walkable from the Yuyuan Garden subway station.

Wukang Road (武康路)

A 1,183-meter street, 12 to 16 meters wide, running from Huashan Road down to Huaihai Middle Road. Opened as Ferguson Road in 1907 in the French Concession, it is lined with Republican-era villas and historic apartments, was listed among Shanghai's 64 streets excluded from widening, and in 2011 was named a China Historical and Cultural Street. Bakeries, coffee shops and boutiques keep afternoon-to-evening hours.

Where: Xuhui District, former French Concession; near Changshu Road and Jiaotong University stations.

Eating and shopping in Shanghai: practical notes

Anchor a food day on two nodes: the Yu Garden and City God Temple quarter for xiaolongbao, xiekehuang and blood soup, where Shanghai Old Street's 1,469 meters of Fangbang Middle Road put most stalls within a 15-minute walk; then Nanjing Road for sit-down pastry and candy shops founded before 1949. Shengjian counters peak before 09:30 — queues at Xiaoyang Shengjian branches are shortest in the first hour after opening.

For shopping, split the visit by street type: Nanjing Road's 1851 avenue covers department stores and flagship outlets between 10:00 and 22:00, while Wukang Road suits an afternoon loop of 1,183 meters past 1907-vintage villas, with coffee shops and independent boutiques. Yu Garden-area stalls close earlier, near 18:00, so schedule snack-buying before dinner.

Frequently Asked Questions

What food is Shanghai most famous for?

Nanxiang xiaolongbao, whose Shanghai school was created by Huang Mingxian at a Jiading-town pastry shop in the late Qing, plus shengjian mantou, which reached Shanghai from Suzhou in the 1920s and had dedicated shops from the 1930s. Pork chop with rice cakes, dated to the 1930s, and crab-shell pastry complete the local canon.

Where should I eat xiaolongbao in Shanghai?

The Yu Garden bazaar near the City God Temple holds the best-known Nanxiang-lineage outlet, with baskets sold from morning onward. District-wide, most old-style breakfast counters carry a version. Expect the longest queues at midday; arriving before 11:00 or after 14:00 shortens the wait.

Which shopping street is better, Nanjing Road or Wukang Road?

Nanjing Road, laid out in 1851 and running to Xizang Middle Road, is the flagship-store avenue labeled 'China's No.1 business street', busiest 10:00–22:00. Wukang Road, opened in 1907 and 1,183 meters long, trades in boutiques, coffee shops and historic architecture; it suits a slower afternoon on foot.

What can I buy at Shanghai Old Street?

The Fangbang Middle Road stretch, built in 1913 by filling a creek, carries pastry stalls, snack vendors and traditional-goods shops along 1,469 meters. Typical buys are fresh-baked xiekehuang, gift boxes of Shanghai sweets and handicraft souvenirs. Most stalls trade daylight hours only, winding down by early evening.

Are there two kinds of shengjian?

Yes. The prevailing 'rich-juice' school mixes pork with skin aspic in a firm dough, while the 'clear-water' school skips the aspic and uses leavened dough for a softer bun. Xiaoyang Shengjian represents the first; Dahuchun and Dongtaixiang the second. Both are breakfast traditions dating to the 1920s–1930s spread of the dish.
