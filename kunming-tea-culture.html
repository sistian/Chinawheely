Overview: Tea Culture in Kunming

Kunming is the trading floor for Yunnan tea: pu-erh pressed into 357-gram cakes and Dianhong, the province's black tea developed in 1939, both move through the city's wholesale tea markets. The mild highland climate - the city sits at about 1,900 metres - doubles as natural storage for aging raw pu-erh, which is why collectors keep warehouses here. A third Yunnan form, the compressed tuocha bowl, was built for the tea-horse caravans and is still pressed in the province. Three styles, one city, and the grading table that separates them.

The tea

Pu-erh (普洱茶)

Pu-erh is a fermented tea from Yunnan, sold as pressed cakes or bricks. Raw (sheng) tea is sun-dried and aged for years, while ripe (shu) tea is piled and turned over weeks of wet fermentation - a technique developed at Kunming's tea factory in 1973 - producing the deep colour and earthy taste that aged cakes are prized for. The standard round cake weighs 357 grams.

How to have it here: Kunming's tea markets pour both styles side by side: boiling water, one rinse, then ten or more short rounds gongfu-style. Cakes, bowl-shaped tuocha and bricks are sold by mountain and by year - Yiwu, Menghai and Lincang are the origin names to read - and spring-harvest cakes carry the clearest premium. Tasting a 2005 cake beside a 2015 cake from the same village is the ordinary way a buyer sets a price, and the pressed inner ticket inside the wrapper is the record of who packed it and when.

Dianhong (滇红)

Dianhong is Yunnan's black tea, made from the large-leaf buds of the region's tea trees and fully oxidised - the standard for Yunnan black tea since it was developed in the 1930s. The first 15 tonnes came out of the Shunning works (today's Fengqing) in 1939 and were shipped to Britain through Hong Kong in the same year.

How to have it here: In Kunming it is the easy daily brew: 90-95°C water, three to four minutes, drunk plain or with milk in the Hong Kong style. Higher grades show golden tips in the leaf, and Fengqing and Lincang are the origin labels worth reading on the pack. A 1939-style recipe is still followed in the Fengqing workshops, where the large-leaf buds from the county's slopes are rolled into tight black strands. The broken-leaf grade, called Gongfu in the trade, is the one used for the fast hotel breakfast cup.

Tuocha (沱茶)

Tuocha is a compressed tea, formed into a bowl or dome shape and packed for transport - the form used to carry tea along the Tea Horse Road towards Tibet. Yunnan is one of the main producing provinces for the shape, and the bowl is still pressed in Kunming-area workshops, either from raw sun-dried leaf or from broken pu-erh swept up after pressing. A common unit weighs 100 grams.

How to have it here: A tuocha of 100 grams unwraps into a small nest that fits a 200 ml pot; it is dosed 5 grams at a time and brewed gongfu-style, with a rinse and eight or nine rounds. Bought by the bowl rather than the cake, it is the practical form for a buyer who wants pu-erh without committing to a 357-gram cake, and the wrapper is scored for the press year. The nest is broken into a piece by piece, usually along the bowl's natural seam, before it goes into the pot.

Tea etiquette in Kunming

Pu-erh is brewed hard: 100°C water, the first 1 or 2 pours discarded as a rinse, then infusions of 10-20 seconds stretched as the rounds climb - a decent cake holds 10 to 15. The small cups are drained in one go, and the host keeps count, announcing the round so nobody pours a spent leaf twice.

Buying carries its own manners: tasting before paying is expected at Kunming's tea markets, and a host will pour several candidate cakes in sequence for comparison. Turning a cake over to check the press date and the inner ticket pressed into the leaf is normal diligence, not rudeness, and bargaining at market stalls is part of the posted game - the same 357-gram cake can be quoted at several prices within a single morning.

Water discipline sets the rhythm. Yunnan households keep a kettle on continuous boil, and a pot left on the heat for an hour is never a complaint - the temperature is a rule of the region rather than a preference. Guests are served in order of age, the pot is never lifted over another cup, and a fresh pot replaces the old one after about 6 rounds rather than being topped up indefinitely.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between sheng and shu pu-erh?

Sheng (raw) is the old way: sun-dried leaf pressed into 357-gram cakes and aged for years to mellow. Shu (ripe) is the shortcut developed at Kunming's tea factory in 1973 - the leaf is piled and turned through weeks of wet fermentation to reach in months a depth that raw tea needs a decade for. Both are sold by year and by mountain of origin.

Why do collectors store cakes in Kunming?

The city sits at about 1,900 metres with mild temperatures year round and a stable humidity pattern, conditions that let raw cakes age slowly without mould. Dealers therefore keep warehouses here, and market vendors will quote a cake's press year - sometimes 10, 20 or more years back - as the main pricing fact.

How do I brew pu-erh correctly?

Boiling water, about 100°C, is the rule. Rinse the leaf once or twice first, then pour short rounds of 10-20 seconds, stretching the time as the rounds climb; a good cake supports ten to fifteen rounds. One portion of leaf in a small pot serves a table, which is why the pour sizes stay small. A 357-gram cake dosed at 7 grams to the round therefore runs to about 50 pours, and the later rounds, drawn from the same leaf, grow longer rather than weaker.

What is Dianhong and how is it drunk?

Yunnan's black tea, fully oxidised and made from large-leaf buds; the first 15 tonnes were produced at Fengqing in 1939 and exported to Britain the same year through Hong Kong. It was developed after 1938, when the eastern Chinese tea districts fell near the war zone and the China Tea Company was ordered to open the southwest growing areas instead. Brew it at 90-95°C for three to four minutes, plain or with milk. Grades with a high share of golden tips sit at the top of the price sheet.

What is tuocha and why is it shaped that way?

Tuocha is compressed into a bowl or dome roughly the size of a teacup, a form built for carrying tea on the Tea Horse Road towards Tibet. A common unit is 100 grams, though 250-gram and 500-gram sizes also exist. Yunnan remains a main producing province, and the wrapper is marked with the press year in the same way a cake is.

How much should a first pu-erh cake cost?

Prices at Kunming's markets are quoted by press year, mountain and storage, so a 10-year-old cake from a named village sits far above a recent one. Tasting three to five candidates before buying is standard, and shops will pour a 100-gram sample of anything they sell. Buy the cake you would drink, not the oldest one on the shelf - a 357-gram cake at 7 grams a round runs to 50 rounds.
