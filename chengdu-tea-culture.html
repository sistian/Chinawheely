Overview: Tea Culture in Chengdu

Chengdu is a tea-drinking city in the fullest sense: the teahouse, not the shop, is the unit of daily life, and a seat with a covered bowl has been part of the city's public furniture for well over a century. The tea itself is the green needle of Mount Emei, roughly 150 km southwest of the city centre, where the bud and the first two leaves are picked together in early spring and sold as flat, glossy green needles. The other half of the culture is the vessel and the room: the covered bowl, three pieces of white porcelain that make a teahouse anywhere in the city, and the open-air tea garden, of which the best known stands inside the city's People's Park. Chengdu itself has held the same name for more than 2,000 years, was capital of the Shu Han kingdom in the 3rd century, and was named a UNESCO city of gastronomy in 2011.

The tea

Zhuyeqing (竹叶青)

Zhuyeqing is a green tea from Mount Emei in Sichuan, sold as flat green needles with a chestnut sweetness. It is picked in early spring, when the bud and the first two leaves are taken together. The mountain stands about 150 km southwest of Chengdu, so the tea arrives in the city as a finished product of an Emei slope, and the picking is tied to the short early-spring window rather than to a season of several harvests. The leaf is notably flat and smooth, and its straightness in the dry form is what separates it on the counter from the curled green teas sold alongside it.

How to have it here: In a Chengdu teahouse it is the standard green brew, dosed by the spoonful into the covered bowl and poured with water near boiling, around 90-95°C, with the first infusion held about 60 seconds and the second a little longer. It is drunk plain, without milk or sugar, and the flat needles are visible through the glass or in the white bowl. Top lots are sold by grade and by picking date, and the early-spring grade carries the clearest premium on the tea counter.

Gongfu tea (工夫茶)

Gongfu cha is a method rather than a tea: a small pot, a small cup, and short pours repeated many times. It is the standard way tea is served in Fujian and Guangdong, and it is why a good pu-erh cake can run to a dozen rounds. The Chengdu table works to the same logic in a different vessel: the teahouse serves from a 150 ml covered bowl and a fairness jug, and each round is poured in small measures so that every guest at the table receives an equal share.

How to have it here: The Chengdu table is built on the covered bowl: about 150 ml of white porcelain, holding roughly 5 grams of leaf, with the lid held slightly ajar to pour and lifted to the nose before the first sip. Water is added from a kettle rather than a pot, and a fairness jug divides each round so that the last drinker served receives the same measure as the first. Refills run to three or four, and the leaf is tipped out only when the host ends the sitting.

Pu-erh (普洱茶)

Pu-erh is a fermented tea from Yunnan, sold as pressed cakes or bricks. The raw tea is sun-dried and then piled and turned over a long period, which produces the deep colour and earthy taste that aged cakes are prized for. It is not a Sichuan tea, but it holds a fixed place on the Chengdu tea table, where a cake is often kept at the counter and a portion of roughly 5 grams weighed out for the table in the same way as the local green.

How to have it here: Served from the clay pot with short pours, the Chengdu method for pu-erh: boiling water, one rinse discarded, then infusions of 10 to 20 seconds that stretch as the rounds climb, with a good cake holding ten to fifteen rounds. Aged cakes are sold by pressing year and by mountain, and the round standard cake weighs 357 grams, a size that divides cleanly for a table of four or five.

Teahouses

Heming Teahouse (鹤鸣茶社)

The Heming Teahouse is the best-known tea garden of the People's Park, the municipal park in the centre of Chengdu, and it works in the open-air form of the Chengdu teahouse: bamboo chairs and low tables under trees, with a 150 ml covered bowl and a thermos jug carried out to the seat. The house has run for decades on a model unchanged in substance since the park teahouses took hold: the price of a seat buys tea for the morning, a customer stays for the length of a day, and the 5 g measure of leaf is set out once for the whole table rather than served to each guest.

Area: People's Park, Chengdu

Tea etiquette in Chengdu

The covered bowl governs the table. It is a 3-piece set of white porcelain, and the lid is both spout and strainer: held slightly ajar it pours, and lifted to the nose it is smelled before the first sip, because a green tea is judged on its liquor first. A guest who has had enough turns the lid back across the bowl; a guest who wants the bowl filled tilts the lid toward the server. Water is poured in a steady stream from a kettle so that the leaf is not disturbed.

The teahouse in Chengdu sells a seat rather than a cup. A table is charged for the tea and the service of the room, and the guest stays as long as the seat is paid for, so that hours pass over one bowl and no one is asked to leave. Nobody hurries a round, and nobody counts the minutes; the bowls are emptied in order as the servers move between the tables. Newcomers are shown a table rather than queued for one, and the tea is already being poured by the time a guest has sat down. The usual session runs a morning, with 3 or 4 rounds over 1 bowl.

Ordering follows the room's own list, and the list is short: green tea, dark tea, and a covered bowl of whatever the house has chosen for the season. The local green, the flat needles of Mount Emei about 150 km away, is the default, and it is drunk plain, without milk and without sugar. Anything served with food is a different arrangement, and the small-cup service of 5 grams in a 150 ml bowl is the standard unit whether the leaf is Emei green or aged Yunnan cake. Once the host ends the sitting, the leaf is discarded rather than recovered with a spoon.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Zhuyeqing and where does it come from?

A green tea from Mount Emei in Sichuan, about 150 km southwest of Chengdu, sold as flat green needles with a chestnut sweetness. It is picked in early spring, when the bud and the first two leaves are taken together. The leaf is notably flat and smooth, which is how it is told apart on the counter from the curled green teas sold next to it.

How is tea served in a Chengdu teahouse?

From the covered bowl: about 150 ml of white porcelain holding roughly 5 grams of leaf, with the lid used as spout and strainer and lifted to the nose before the first sip. The local green is brewed at around 90-95°C, the first infusion held about 60 seconds, and refills run to three or four. There is no milk and no sugar.

What is the role of the People's Park teahouse?

It is the city's reference for how tea rooms are organised: the open-air form, with bamboo chairs and low tables under trees, tea carried out to the seat and the price of a seat buying tea for the morning. The best-known of them is the Heming Teahouse, and the practice of spending a whole day over one 150 ml bowl of tea is the pattern the capital's tea gardens are known for.

Does Chengdu have its own tea, or does it sell other provinces' tea?

Both. The city's own is the flat green needle of Mount Emei, 150 km away, picked in early spring. At the same time the standard tea counter also carries aged Yunnan cakes, and pu-erh is brewed in the Chengdu fashion with a clay pot: boiling water, one rinse, then short pours of 10 to 20 seconds across ten to fifteen rounds, from a standard 357-gram cake.

How does a Chengdu teahouse charge, and how long can I stay?

The room sells a seat rather than a cup: the table is charged for the tea and the service, and a guest stays for as long as the seat is paid for, often a whole morning. There is no per-round charge and no expectation that the table turns over quickly. Orders are placed for the table rather than individually, so the whole group drinks from the same 150 ml bowl, dosed with about 5 grams of leaf.

Why does Chengdu use a covered bowl instead of a teapot?

The covered bowl is the three-piece vessel of the city's teahouse tradition: a bowl that holds the leaf, a lid that strains and pours, and a saucer to stand on. The lid is lifted to the nose before the first sip because a green tea is judged on its liquor, and the bowl's volume of about 150 ml with roughly 5 grams of leaf sets the standard measure for every table. A fairness jug divides each round so that the last guest served drinks the same as the first.
