Overview: Eating and Shopping in Chengdu

Chengdu's snack canon is unusually well dated: dan dan noodles trace to an 1841 Zigong street vendor, fuqi feipian to a 1930s husband-and-wife stall, and Long Chaoshou to a shop opened in 1941. This page lists six such foods with their documented origins and three commercial streets with construction dates from 1718 to 1924.

What to eat

Dan dan noodles (担担面)

A noodle dish named for the shoulder pole it was sold from, credited to a Zigong vendor nicknamed Chen Baobao in 1841 and later carried into Chengdu and Chongqing. Original hawkers used a two-compartment copper pot — noodles boiling in one, braised pork trotter stewing in the other. Thin noodles come dressed in chili oil, sesame paste, minced pork, preserved vegetable, crushed peanut and Sichuan pepper.

Where: A citywide standard; the oldest documented versions sit in the old-town snack restaurants around Chunxi Road rather than any single stall.

Fuqi feipian (couple's lung slices) (夫妻肺片)

A cold beef dish from Chengdu. Late-Qing vendors in the city's Hui quarter sold cheap boiled offal slices dressed in soy, chili oil and ground sesame; in the 1930s the stall of Guo Chaohua and Zhang Tianzheng, one making and one selling, gave the dish its 'couple' name. Despite the label, today's plate holds beef head skin, heart, tongue and tripe — no lung — cut large and thin.

Where: Sold at cold-dish counters citywide; the style descends from the Hui-quarter vendors near what is now the old city center.

Long Chaoshou wontons (龙抄手)

Chaoshou is the Sichuan word for wonton, and Long Chaoshou is the shop Zhang Guangwu opened in Chengdu in 1941, named as a pun on the Nonghua tea garden. Its wontons use exceptionally thin, near-translucent skins. After moving to 6–8 South Chunxi Road in the 1960s it grew into a large snack restaurant serving the full local repertoire, and in 1995 received the China Time-Honored Brand title.

Where: The flagship remains at 6–8 South Chunxi Road, inside the Chunxi pedestrian system.

Dan hong gao griddle cakes (蛋烘糕)

A Chengdu street snack cooked from carts: batter of egg and flour poured into a special small pan, fried into a round, folded over filling into a half-moon. Original stuffings were cold 'three shreds' salad, minced pork with cowpea, or sugar with sesame; later versions added jam and pork floss. Sold by the piece within minutes of cooking.

Where: Pushcart vendors concentrate around school gates and lanes of the old city; Jinli street and Kuanzhai alleys keep fixed stalls selling through evening hours.

Chuanchuan skewer hot pot (串串香)

Skewered street hot pot: ingredients sliced and threaded onto bamboo sticks, cooked by the customer in a shared málà broth and paid for by the stick. The format appeared on Sichuan streets in the 1980s–1990s after reform and opening, out of Leshan's málàtang tradition; the same lineage produced cold-pot chuanchuan, maocai and bobo chicken. Counted per skewer, a sitting runs to dozens of sticks.

Where: Dedicated chuanchuan restaurants cluster in the older districts east of the city center; Jinli's stalls offer tourist-sized versions.

Málà hot pot (麻辣火锅)

The thick-broth school of Chinese hot pot: a heavily spiced, oil-based málà soup kept boiling at the table while diners cook sliced meat, offal, vegetables, tofu and noodles piece by piece — the defining 'cook and eat at once' method of the form. Broth is split into mild and fiery halves on request. Ordering runs by the plate; a standard table works through fifteen to twenty plates.

Where: Hot-pot houses operate across the city at every price class; the densest rows of old-brand shops sit within two kilometers of Chunxi Road.

Where to shop

Chunxi Road (春熙路)

Chengdu's busiest commercial street, built by warlord Yang Sen in 1924 and composed of four pedestrian sections — east, west, south and north — that bend at Zhongshan Square because a pharmacy owner refused demolition. The wider Chunxi zone covers roughly 0.4 square kilometers between Zongfu Road, East Upper Street, Hongxing Road and Yanshikou, ranking among the old city's three main shopping districts. Stores trade until about 22:00.

Where: Jinjiang District, city center; multiple Metro Line 2 and 3 exits open directly onto the pedestrian streets.

Kuanzhai Alleys (宽窄巷子)

Three parallel east-west lanes — Wide Alley, Narrow Alley and Well Alley — with the courtyard houses between them, surviving from the Manchu garrison city built in 1718 after the Qing campaign against the Dzungars, when over a thousand banner troops were stationed in Chengdu. Listed in the first batch of national demonstration pedestrian streets and a national night-time culture and tourism zone; teahouses, snack stalls and craft shops fill the lanes.

Where: Qingyang District, off Changshun Street; about two kilometers west of Tianfu Square.

Jinli Street (锦里)

A heritage commercial street beside the Wuhou Shrine, restored and run by the shrine museum, on a site whose commercial history is traced to the Qin and Han eras, when 'Jinli' was already a byword for the brocade city of Chengdu. The present pedestrian lane mixes Three-Kingdoms themes with western-Sichuan crafts and snacks; stall hours extend into the evening.

Where: Wuhou District, adjacent to the Wuhou Shrine; reachable via Metro Line 3 with a short walk.

Eating and shopping in Chengdu: practical notes

Sequence the snack list geographically: Long Chaoshou's 1941 flagship sits at 6–8 South Chunxi Road, dan dan noodles and fuqi feipian appear on most menus within the 0.4-square-kilometer Chunxi zone, and dan hong gao carts work the lanes nearer the schools. For chuanchuan and hot pot, budget a dozen to twenty skewers or plates per person and expect the málà broth to arrive split mild and hot unless you specify one.

Two heritage streets cover the shopping half-day: Kuanzhai Alleys, whose lanes preserve the 1718 garrison-city layout, runs teahouses and craft shops into the evening as a national night-time zone, while Jinli beside the Wuhou Shrine leans on its Qin-Han trading pedigree for souvenir snacks. Chunxi Road stays the practical base — four pedestrian sections built from 1924 with late closing hours and metro exits at the doorstep.

Frequently Asked Questions

What food is Chengdu most famous for?

A dated snack roster: dan dan noodles from an 1841 Zigong vendor, fuqi feipian from a 1930s couple's stall, and Long Chaoshou wontons from a shop founded in 1941. Skewered chuanchuan hot pot, which spread from Leshan's málàtang in the 1980s–1990s, plus málà hot pot itself, complete the contemporary canon.

Where is the best place to try multiple Chengdu snacks at once?

Long Chaoshou at 6–8 South Chunxi Road has operated as a multi-dish snack restaurant since the 1960s. Jinli and Kuanzhai Alleys concentrate stalls but at tourist pricing. For dan hong gao, street carts around the old city sell fresher pieces for less.

What is fuqi feipian, and does it contain lung?

It is a cold dish of beef and offal — head skin, heart, tongue and tripe — sliced thin and tossed in chili oil, soy and ground sesame. Despite the name 'lung slices', modern recipes use no lung. The dish descends from late-Qing Hui-quarter vendors and took its 'couple' name from the 1930s stall of Guo Chaohua and Zhang Tianzheng.

Which shopping street should I choose in Chengdu?

Chunxi Road, opened in 1924 and spanning four pedestrian streets within a 0.4-square-kilometer zone, handles department stores and late hours. Kuanzhai Alleys, laid out under the 1718 Manchu garrison, suits teahouses and crafts. Jinli, beside the Wuhou Shrine, trades on Three-Kingdoms theming and stays lively at night.

How spicy is Chengdu hot pot really?

The broth is chili-and-Sichuan-pepper based by default, but tables can order a divided pot with one mild half. Chuanchuan skewers are priced individually, so heat exposure is controllable by what you cook. Ordering rice milk or a plain soup alongside is the common local practice.
