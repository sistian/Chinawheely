Overview: Photography in Wuhan

Wuhan's photographic identity comes from its rivers and its post-war concrete. The Yangtze River Bridge is the first thing China built across its own great river, and it still photographs like 1950s infrastructure, all rivets and lattice. The old city in Wuchang has the yellow crane tower on its hill, the yellow-tiled pavilions and painted eaves that are the city's most distinctive traditional architecture, and a breakfast alley where the steam is the subject.

Yellow Crane Tower (黄鹤楼)

The current tower is 51.4 m high and covers 3,219 square metres, rebuilt between 1981 and 1985 at a site about 1 km from where the original stood on Snake Hill. It has five storeys with a distinctive gilded shanzi roof at the top, and the first floor carries a ceramic mural of white clouds and yellow cranes, with a couplet on flanking columns whose individual lines run up to 7 m. The best frames come from the riverside to the west and from the terraces inside the tower looking out over the confluence of the Han and Yangtze rivers.

best light: golden hour · landmark

Getting there: Metro Line 5 to Huanghelou station. The tower complex includes a cable car from the base up Snake Hill, and the riverside promenade below is a short walk west along Wuchang Bund.

  • The classic composition is from the Yangtze riverfront to the northwest, where the tower sits above the trees with the bridge line running across the water below.
  • The cable car climbs from the ticket gate to the tower, which saves the walk and gives a view on the way up.
  • Late afternoon puts warm light on the west-facing eaves with the river behind, which is better than the front-on approach.

Wuhan Yangtze River Bridge (武汉长江大桥)

Built between 1955 and 1957, this is the first permanent road and rail crossing of the Yangtze anywhere, and it is 1.6 km long, running from Turtle Hill in Hanyang to Snake Hill in Wuchang on eight piers with a double-deck steel truss. What makes it a strong subject is the truss itself: a repeating web of diagonal steel members that frames the sky and the river, and which reads as a distinctly 1950s Chinese industrial aesthetic. At the Hanyang end, the 1957 memorial monument stands with the worker heroes who built the cofferdam, and the low riverside park at both ends gives clean platforms.

best light: blue hour · modern

Getting there: The pedestrian walkway has access at both ends; the western entrance is off the Hanyang riverfront road near the monument. Metro Line 6 to Dongan or a taxi from the city centre gets you close.

  • Shooting from the deck at blue hour with the city lights on both banks gives the best result, since the truss is a dark frame against a lit sky.
  • The Hanyang monument terrace is the cleanest platform for a symmetrical shot straight along the bridge.
  • A long exposure smooths the moving traffic on the lower deck into light streaks under the truss.

Tingtao and the East Lake Causeway (东湖听涛景区)

The Tingtao area on the north-west shore of East Lake is where the city meets the water, and the causeway there is lined with mature trees whose branches meet overhead, making a tunnel effect along the water. East Lake covers 33 square kilometres with 100 km of shoreline, so the scale is completely different from a city park, and the mornings when the mist sits on the water between the willows are the reason to be there at dawn. The white pagoda on the shore, visible across the water from several points, gives a traditional element to a landscape otherwise made of modern construction.

best light: sunrise · nature

Getting there: Drive to the Tingtao scenic area gate on the north-west shore, or use the Metro Line 8 to Yinyu Road and walk the last stretch. Bicycles and electric scooters are available at the gate and the lakeside roads are car-free.

  • Sunrise with the mist on the water and the causeway trees in silhouette is the defining image here.
  • A long lens from the causeway isolates the white pagoda across the water with the city behind it.
  • The area is busy at weekends, so weekday sunrise is worth the early start.

Hubu Alley at Breakfast (户部巷)

Hubu Alley is 150 m long and 4 m wide, though it was widened to 8 m in 2006 with 8 million yuan of investment and its overhead cabling buried, and it is a genuine Ming and Qing era lane of brick-and-timber houses in Wuchang. Its photographic subject is the breakfast trade: the steam off the woks, the queue at Shi Popo's hot dry noodle counter, the paper and plastic, and the condensation on the sheeting over each stall. The light in a lane this narrow is always soft and indirect, which is exactly why it flatters food and faces.

best light: all day · street-life

Getting there: Metro Line 7 to Hubu Alley station, with the lane directly above the exit. It is 4 km from the city centre and about 15 minutes on foot from Yellow Crane Tower.

  • Arrive before 8 am to have the crowds and the activity; by 10 am the stalls are closing.
  • Shoot with the lens at roughly chest height so the steam catches the light against the darker lane behind.
  • The old lane runs north-south, so the light comes in sideways from the east in the morning.

Zenghou Yi Chime Bells at the Hubei Provincial Museum (湖北省博物馆)

The bells themselves are a photographer's subject: 65 bronze bells from 433 BC, in descending order of size across two rows, each with a distinct tone covering three octaves and a half and a pitch notation inscribed on its body more than 2,000 years ago. They are displayed in a darkened gallery, and a low ambient light rakes across the patina and the inscriptions, so a longer exposure and a wider aperture bring out the surface detail that a fast shutter would lose. The museum building's own form is the second subject, its glazed roof lantern modelled on the bronze drum.

best light: all day · modern

Getting there: Inside the East Lake Scenic Area on the west shore. Metro Line 8 to Hubei Museum station. The museum is free but requires advance registration and is closed on Mondays.

  • The gallery is kept deliberately dim, so a tripod or a braced long exposure is needed to hold the exposure for the bronze detail.
  • The atrium's daylight lantern is best photographed in the middle of the day when the column of light is strong.
  • If the weekly chime bell performance is running, the players and the bell frames make strong motion and silhouette frames.

Moshan Hill and the Lingxiu Temple (磨山)

Moshan rises to 120 m on the western shore of East Lake and carries the 600-year-old Lingxiu Temple, the oldest building in Wuhan, together with a botanical garden of more than 72 hectares and over 4,000 plant species, and a sculpture collection of more than 100 works spread across the hillsides. The reason to photograph it is the sequence: the hill forms a wooded backdrop to the lake, the temple roofs catch the last light through the trees, and the sculptures are positioned to be discovered on the paths rather than lined up in a plaza.

best light: golden hour · nature

Getting there: Enter the Moshan scenic area from the east side of the lake. A car can reach the lower entrance and the internal shuttle bus takes you up to the temple and the sculpture paths.

  • Late afternoon sun comes from the west across the lake, which backlights the tree line and lights the temple roofs from behind.
  • The hill paths climb enough that the view back over the lake opens up as you go, which is worth the effort for one frame.
  • The botanical garden's glasshouses are lit and make an interesting modern frame among the trees.

Hanyang Riverfront and the Guiyuan Temple Quarter (汉阳江滩)

The Hanyang riverbank between the Yangtze Bridge and the Guiyuan Temple is where the old treaty-port worker city meets the modern riverside park, and it has the best combination of working river life and warm lights in the city. The fishing boats on the moorings, the iron bridge overhead, the restored Qintai theatre and the shophouse facades of the old Hanyang street give layers of period without a theme park. At dusk the bridge structure catches the last light while the shore lighting comes on, and the river carries both, which is what makes this a working blue-hour subject rather than a static waterfront.

best light: blue hour · street-life

Getting there: The riverfront park runs south from the Yangtze Bridge along the Hanyang bank. Metro Line 6 to Hanyang or a taxi; the park itself is walkable and flat.

  • Frame the bridge from the southern end of the riverbank so the whole 1.6 km span runs diagonally across the shot.
  • The restored old Hanyang street behind Guiyuan Temple is worth walking for the shophouse fronts.
  • Blue hour, when the bridge is still lit by daylight on top but the shore lights are on, is the useful overlap.

Photographing a river city

Wuhan is a hard subject for one technical reason: the air over the rivers carries a lot of moisture and city aerosol, and the result is that distant objects lose contrast faster than in drier cities. The Huang Crane Tower on its hill is a classic example, often appearing washed out from across the water when it looks crisp from the west bank. The correction is not to filter it out but to expose for it, which means checking the histogram on the tower rather than on the bright sky behind it.

A polariser is more valuable here than in most cities. It removes the surface glare off the Yangtze and Han rivers, which otherwise eats a stop and a half of light and washes out any reflection, and it does the same job on the wet paving of the riverfront parks after rain, which is common in this climate.

For the food and street work in Hubu Alley, the light is naturally soft and narrow, so the challenge is exposure and depth of field rather than the light itself. A fast prime wide open at a raised ISO handles the dim interiors of the covered galleries, and the new Hubei Provincial Museum's dim bronze gallery is the one place where a tripod is genuinely necessary.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best spot for the classic Yellow Crane Tower photograph?

From the northwest side, on the Wuchang riverfront below the hill, which puts the tower above the trees with the Yangtze and the bridge line running across the bottom of the frame. The tower is 51.4 m tall on Snake Hill and is visible from a wide area, but this angle gives the cleanest separation from the surrounding city. Late afternoon, when the west-facing eaves are lit, is better than the front-on midday approach.

Can I walk onto the Wuhan Yangtze River Bridge?

Yes, there is a pedestrian walkway with access from both the Hanyang and Wuchang ends, and it is the only way to be on the deck with the river 40 m below. It spans 1.6 km between Turtle Hill and Snake Hill. Access hours vary and the walkway closes periodically for maintenance, so check locally. The blue-hour photo from the deck, with the truss dark against a lit sky, is the strongest version.

How early should I go to Hubu Alley?

Between 6:30 and 9 am. The lane is 150 m long and 4 m wide, was widened to 8 m in 2006, and functions as Wuhan's breakfast street, with the mianwo and hot dry noodle stalls operating from around 5:30 in the morning. After 9:30 the shutters come down and the street empties. The narrow lane also means the light is always soft and indirect, so it photographs well in overcast conditions.

Is the chime bell gallery in the Hubei Provincial Museum hard to photograph?

It is the one genuinely difficult interior in the city, because the gallery is deliberately dim to protect the bronze. The bells are 65 pieces from 433 BC with inscriptions more than 2,000 years old, and the detail worth photographing is only visible with a slow shutter. A tripod is the sensible answer, and if one is not allowed, a high ISO with careful focus on the nearest bell will still give you the shape and the rows.

Which Wuhan location is best for a wide landscape at sunrise?

East Lake, specifically the Tingtao causeway on the north-west shore. The lake covers 33 square kilometres, so the scale is genuine, and the causeway trees make a natural tunnel over the water with mist on the surface at dawn. The wooded hills of Moshan on the opposite western shore give you a far bank, which stops the image from being just water and trees.

Are drone flights possible over the Yangtze in Wuhan?

It is complicated, because there are two bridges, a river port and approaches to the airport in the airspace, and the riverfront parks near the Yangtze Bridge have restrictions. Permission is effectively needed for anything over the water. The bridge deck and the Hanyang riverfront give good raised viewpoints without it, and in this hazy river air a low angle often beats an aerial one.
