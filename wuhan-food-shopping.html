Overview: Eating and Shopping in Wuhan

Wuhan eats breakfast standing up. The word for the morning meal is guozao, and the city's short queue of stalls still sells sesame-pasted noodles, rice-studded bean skins and clay-pot broths from early morning. Wuhan is the capital of Hubei, a city of 13.77 million people across 8,569 square kilometres split by the Yangtze and its Han tributary into the three old towns of Wuchang, Hankou and Hanyang, and the food culture reflects all three. It is also the trading floor of central China: the city's wholesale street, duck-neck brands and century-old noodle shops are known far beyond the province.

What to eat

Hot Dry Noodles (热干面)

Alkaline noodles are boiled to about 90 percent, drained, drenched in sesame oil and left to cool, then tossed at the last moment with sesame paste, soy sauce, pickled radish and scallion. The oil-coated noodles do not congeal, which is why the dish is assembled in seconds at the counter. The story credits a street seller named Li Bao in the 1920s, who oiled noodles left over from a morning's trading. Wuhan's version is counted with Shanxi knife-cut noodles, Lanzhou beef noodles, Sichuan dan dan noodles and northern zhajiang noodles as one of China's five famous noodles.

Where: Cai Lin Ji, first opened in 1930 on Man Chun Road and now at 726-728 Zhongshan Avenue, has more than 100 branches in Hubei. It moved to Hubu Alley in 2009 and back to Jianghan Road in 2010, and was named one of China's ten famous noodles in 2016.

Three-Delicate Bean Skin (三鲜豆皮)

A four-hundred-year-old breakfast built in a wide iron pan. The wrapper is mung bean flour blended with egg and rice batter, spread thin over the hot pan; underneath goes a layer of glutinous rice with diced shiitake, pork and bamboo shoot, the three delicacies of the name. The parcel is covered with a second skin, steamed for several minutes, then finished with spring onion and cut into squares of five to eight centimetres, three to six to a plate. The wrapper sets slightly crisp against the soft, sticky rice inside.

Where: Lao Tong Cheng on Minzhu Road is the shop most visitors are pointed to; the original main building, which once hosted Mao Zedong, has since been demolished and rebuilt. The dish is also sold across the Wuhan city circle and in Enshi.

Wuchang Fish (武昌鱼)

A species rather than a recipe: the blunt snout bream, Megalobrama amblycephala, is farmed in Liangzi Lake on the boundary between Ezhou and Jiangxia. The fish was named scientifically in 1955 by the ecologist Yi Bolu, and reaches about 35 centimetres in length. The county gazetteer records the judgement that the catch of Fankou, where the current turns back on itself, surpasses that of anywhere else, and holds that a true specimen has a pale belly with no black membrane. It is one of the most important aquaculture species worldwide.

Where: Ezhou and Jiangxia are the production area rather than Wuhan proper, so the fish is usually bought at restaurants rather than in the city. Chefs favour steamed preparations with ginger and scallion, which keep the flesh tender.

Lotus Root and Pork Rib Soup (排骨藕汤)

The centrepiece of Wuhan simmered soup, a slow dish rather than a quick one. Ribs and lotus root go into a black earthenware pot known as a diaozi and simmer over low heat for several hours. Winter lotus root is preferred because its starch content is highest then and it turns powdery rather than fibrous. Radish and kelp are also used, with winter melon and shiitake in some versions. Traditionally it was a dish for festivals and honoured guests rather than an everyday bowl.

Where: Xiao Tao Yuan, opened in 1934 on Lanling Road in Hankou, is the reference shop; its head cook Yu Fengshan was known as the king of simmered soup and his son opened his own version on Qiuchang Road.

Mianwo (面窝)

A ring of batter fried in a dedicated iron mould, crisp outside and soft and cake-like inside, and one of the few Wuhan breakfasts eaten with the hands. It is sold by the piece at street stalls from the first kettle of the morning, and the version with sesame and scallion in the batter is the standard. Because it travels well and keeps its texture for an hour, it is the breakfast that gets eaten on the way to work rather than at a table.

Where: Xie's stall in Hubu Alley has been selling them since the 1940s, and the lane is known as the first breakfast street of the city for Han-flavoured food.

Zhou Hei Ya (周黑鸭)

The city's most successful export is a braised duck neck with a sweet-spicy peppercorn dressing, sold in vacuum-sealed boxes and eaten as a snack on trains and flights. The business started in Wuhan in 1995 and began retail under the name Zhouji Guweiyia in 1997; the Zhou Hei Ya trademark was registered in 2005 and the company followed in 2006. It was named one of China's best-known trademarks in 2012, listed in Hong Kong in 2016, and in 2011 it bought the naming rights to the Jianghan Road station on Metro Line 2.

Where: Shops run across Hubei and most major mainland cities; the shops on Jianghan Road and around the transport hubs are the ones that carry the range of skewered cuts rather than just the neck.

Where to shop

Jianghan Road (江汉路)

The central retail street, 1,550 metres long, running from the Jianghan Customs House clock tower on the riverside up to Zhongshan Avenue and on to Jiefang Avenue. It began as a 300-metre dirt track, was metalled in 1861 after Hankou was opened as a treaty port, and was named Xinsheng Road in 1901 for Liu Xinsheng, who donated the low-lying ground inside the old city wall to the concession. It was renamed Jianghan Road in 1927 and was known as little Hong Kong in the 1930s, when watchmakers and pharmacies already lined it.

Where: Jiang'an and Jianghan districts, on the Hankou side of the Yangtze. Part of the length stays open to traffic, so it is busier in the evening than at midday.

Hanzheng Street (汉正街)

Three point two kilometres long and five to seven metres wide, running east from Yudaimen to the junction with Minzu Road, and still called the foremost street under heaven. It formed when the Han river shifted course in the Ming period and a new town grew on the north bank of the new channel, with wharves linked end to end. Eight wholesale trades dominated it in the Qing dynasty, handling money, pawning, metals, candles, silk, cloth, lacquer and paper, and traders from Shanxi, Shaanxi, Sichuan, Hunan, Jiangxi, Zhejiang and Jiangsu settled along it.

Where: Qiaokou district. It remains a small-goods wholesale centre supplying clothing, light manufactures, porcelain and small appliances across central China; a relocation to the Hankou North market was planned from 2010.

Hubu Alley (户部巷)

A 150-metre alley four metres wide in the old Wuchang city, named for the Ministry of Revenue office that once stood beside it and already recorded in the Huangguang Atlas of the Jiajing era in the Ming dynasty. Because it stood by the long-distance wharves on the Yangtze, hawkers and vendors gathered there and the lane became known as the first breakfast street of Han-flavoured food. It declined after the nationalisation of the food trade in the 1950s and was rebuilt by the Wuchang district in 2002.

Where: Wuchang district, off Minzhu Road and reached from the Wuchang side of the river rather than from Hankou.

Jiqing Street (吉庆街)

The evening counterpart to Hubu Alley: a food street whose stalls open after dark, with the local shorthand that you eat in Hubu Alley in the morning and on Jiqing Street at night. It keeps the same mix of grill smoke and street seating that made the breakfast lanes famous, but with the emphasis on late sitting and drinking rather than on queues for noodles. Several of the older vendors moved here when the breakfast trade was reorganised.

Where: Jiang'an district, close to the ferry side of Hankou and within walking distance of Jianghan Road.

Eating and shopping in Wuhan

Breakfast is a working meal rather than a sit-down one. Between roughly 6 and 9 in the morning the counters along Hubu Alley and the riverside lanes do most of their volume, and the queue moves fast. Noodles are mixed at the moment of ordering, so eat where you buy, or at least stand within sight of the counter if you take away, because the sesame paste is folded through the noodles only once.

Two practical warnings about shopping. Hanzheng Street is a wholesale market: prices are per carton and the counters expect serious buyers, so a single customer asking for one item will not get served well. Jianghan Road is the opposite, a retail street where the same brands are sold per piece, and where the shops are open late into the evening. For the braised duck, buy the boxed versions rather than eating it in the street, since the shop and the street are two different kinds of experience in this city.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is guozao and when is it eaten?

Guozao is the Wuhan word for the morning meal, and it is a set of small, fast dishes rather than one breakfast. Noodles, bean skins, fried rings and simmered soup are eaten between about 6 and 9 in the morning, usually standing at a counter or at a low plastic stool. The tradition is so established that the city counts breakfast as a proper meal rather than a snack, and the lanes around Hubu Alley are busy well before the shops nearby open.

Which noodle shop is the one to try?

Cai Lin Ji is the name almost every visitor is given. It began in 1930 as a single frontage on Man Chun Road named after two trees that grew outside the Cai family home, became state-run in 1966, merged with the Hou Shengli restaurant in 1996, and by then was selling up to 450 kilograms of noodles a day from 490 square metres over four floors. The 2016 designation as one of China's ten famous noodles is the most recent addition to a list of provincial and national honours.

Is the food on Hubu Alley good, or is it a tourist street?

It is a genuine breakfast street that happens to be well known. The lane is only 150 metres long and four metres wide, and it grew up around the wharves rather than around visitors. Mianwo stalls have worked there since the 1940s, and the 2002 rebuild kept the Ming and Qing street proportions with the food stalls on the pavement. Early morning is the only time worth going; by midday the lane is quiet and most counters have closed.

What should I buy on Hanzheng Street?

Small goods: clothing, light manufactures, porcelain and small appliances, sold at wholesale prices to shops in Hubei and the surrounding provinces. The street is 3.2 kilometres long with eight long-established trades, and the goods are the same ones the street has carried since the Ming period, when it formed beside the new channel of the Han river. Individual visitors are better served on Jianghan Road, which sells the same kinds of goods by the piece.

Why is Wuchang fish not really a Wuhan dish?

The species is named for the old Wuchang county, but it is farmed in Liangzi Lake, where Ezhou and Jiangxia meet on the far side of the lake from the city. The modern name was only settled in 1955, when the fish scientist Yi Bolu gave the blunt snout bream its scientific name. It counts among the most important aquaculture species in the world, and it is usually encountered in the city as a steamed dish in a restaurant rather than as something you buy on a street corner.
