Overview: Photography in Shanghai

Shanghai photographs in two completely different registers. On one side, the Bund and Lujiazui give you the pre-war international architecture and the 632 m Shanghai Tower stacked against it across 113 km of Huangpu River, which is the single most demanding skyline contrast in China. On the other, the French Concession's plane trees and the canal lanes of the water towns give you a slower, more intimate street frame that most visitors never walk far enough to find.

The Bund (外滩)

The Bund is a 1.5 km stretch of road along the Huangpu containing more than twenty buildings in competing styles, from Renaissance and eclectic to early modernist, which is why it is called the exhibition of world architecture. The best frames are not from the promenade itself but from the north end near Waibaidu Bridge, where the old HSBC building and the Customs House stack against the Lujiazui towers. Shoot from the Pudong side at the far end of the east Bund for the compressed telephoto version with all three towers overlapping. Golden hour turns the older stone facades a warm ochre while the glass towers stay cold blue.

best light: golden hour · landmark

Getting there: Park under the Bund Source mall on Zhongshan East 1st Road and use the elevated walkway, or take Line 10 to Yuyuan Garden and walk south. A car can drop you at the north end near the Waibaidu Bridge, which is the most useful single position for the classic skyline.

  • Stand on the stone terrace at the north end rather than in the middle of the promenade, where trees block the classic view.
  • A long lens at 200 mm from the Pudong side compresses the towers into a single dense block, which is the shot most people want.
  • The illumination comes on around 7 pm in winter and 8:30 pm in summer, and the crowd density roughly doubles once it does.

Yu Garden and Yuyuan Bazaar (豫园)

The garden was built in 1559 by Pan Yundian, a Sichuan provincial treasurer, as a private retreat for his parents, and it covers over 70 mu with Yulinglong, one of the three most famous rocks in Jiangnan, a 3.3 m porous limestone prized since the Ming. The surrounding Yuyuan Bazaar is the oldest commercial street in Shanghai and its red lanterns strung across the lane above the food stalls are the city's signature night image. The photography works best in the hour after sunset when the lantern light is still on but the sky has not gone fully black.

best light: blue hour · street-life

Getting there: Line 10 to Yuyuan Garden or Line 8 to Old City God Temple. The main entrance is on Anren Street off the Yuyuan New Road, and a car can drop passengers at the corner of Yuyuan New Road and Fuyou Road.

  • The garden itself closes in the evening, so the lantern bazaar is a separate, busier experience from the garden during the day.
  • Shoot the lanterns from the small bridge inside the bazaar rather than from the main commercial street, which is usually congested.
  • The best stone-carving detail is on the Yulinglong rock, which is lit separately and glows after dark.

Lujiazui and Shanghai Tower (陆家嘴)

Shanghai Tower is 632 m tall and 128 storeys, the tallest building in China and the third tallest in the world, with an observation deck on floor 118 at 546 m. Its shape is a twisting spiral, and the 24 sky gardens set into its curtain wall break up the façade in a way that no other tower in the cluster does. From the opposite bank the three towers form the most photographed skyline in the country. Sunset and the twenty minutes after are when the glass catches the last light while the sky behind still holds colour.

best light: sunset · modern

Getting there: Line 2 to Lujiazui, or the Pudong-side Bund promenade. A car is useful only for the Pudong riverside, where parking is along the Binjiang Avenue and paid by the hour.

  • The best elevated viewpoint is the north end of the Pudong riverside near the Ocean Aquarium, where you can get all three towers in one frame.
  • Inside Shanghai Tower the shuttle elevators run at 18 m per second, which is disorienting in the best way.
  • Shooting from the Pudong side at blue hour lets you include the lit Bund in the foreground and the towers behind.

Wukang Road and the French Concession (武康路)

Wukang Road is 1,183 m long and 12 to 16 m wide, running from Huashan Road to Huaihai Middle Road through what was the French Concession, and it is lined with garden villas and shikumen houses built between the 1910s and the 1930s. The sycamores planted in the 1930s have become the defining feature of the street. From 2013 Xuhui district deliberately stopped sweeping the fallen leaves along the Wukang Road to Anfu Road block, creating a leaf-viewing street that draws heavy crowds every November. Low golden sun filtering sideways through the trees makes the leaves glow from behind.

best light: golden hour · street-life

Getting there: Line 10 to Jiaotong University or Line 1 to Changshu Road, both a short walk away. Driving is discouraged because the street has no practical parking; use a taxi drop at the Huaihai Middle Road end.

  • The junction at Huaihai Middle Road where the Wukang building frames the road is the single most reproduced view in the district.
  • The former residence of Ba Jin at No. 113 and the Normandie Apartments at No. 135 and 137 are all signed heritage buildings.
  • Between mid-November and the first week of December the leaf fall peaks, and early morning before 8 am is the only quiet hour.

Jing'an Temple (静安寺)

Jing'an is one of the four major Buddhist temples of Shanghai and its history runs back to 247 CE, when the monk Kang Senghui founded it on the north bank of the Wusong River under Sun Quan. It was rebuilt on its present site in 1216 after the original foundation washed away, and the gilt-bronze tower that stands on the roof today is 30 m of gilded metal with 17 tiers. The hall interior carries a 3.87 m carved jade Buddha, and the surrounding Sculpture Park is one of the few places in the city where old lilac trees and contemporary outdoor galleries share the same block.

best light: blue hour · night

Getting there: Line 2 and Line 7 to Jing'an Temple, with the temple directly at the station exit. Parking is available in the adjacent mall garage.

  • The main gate faces Nanjing West Road and is busiest at dusk, so the side gate on the Sculpture Park gives a cleaner architectural frame.
  • Inside, the Jade Buddha chamber is usually roped off and lit warmly, which makes a good interior exposure bracket.
  • The temple is active and functioning, so dress respectfully and keep voices down in the halls.

Shanghai Museum (上海博物馆)

The museum building, opened on 12 October 1996, is a study in round heaven and square earth, and it works as a photo subject in its own right with strong overhead daylight and geometric framing. But the real reason to bring a camera is the collection and the architecture of the galleries: the bronze gallery alone displays over 400 pieces across 1,200 square metres, and the new East Building, opened in 2024, holds twelve galleries including the rare 'see the light through' bronze mirror. Entry to the permanent galleries has been free since 10 March 2008, though a reservation is required for the popular galleries.

best light: all day · modern

Getting there: Line 1 to People's Square, or Line 2 to East Nanjing Road, both minutes away. The People's Square entrance has no parking; use the museum's own underground garage via Xizang Middle Road.

  • The bronze gallery is dimly lit by design to protect the patina, so a high ISO and a slow shutter on a tripod is the way in.
  • The east hall's 6,000 square metre atrium works well for a wide symmetrical upward shot.
  • Major travelling exhibitions require separate tickets and separate booking, and sell out well ahead.

Huangpu River and Nanpu Bridge (黄浦江)

The Huangpu is 113 km long and divides Shanghai, and its curve is what makes the city legible: from almost any riverside point you get both the colonial Bund and the Lujiazui towers in the same view. The ferries that cross it for a few yuan give you a moving platform for shooting the Bund from the water. The best elevated night position is around Nanpu Bridge on the Puxi side, where the spiral ramp climbs high enough to look down the river with the towers off to the right, and the working cargo and commuter traffic on the water gives scale.

best light: blue hour · night

Getting there: Public ferries run from the Bund wharf near the North Bund to South Pudong and Lujiazui for a few yuan each. Nanpu Bridge is on the Inner Ring with no public access; a taxi drop at the Shanghai Ocean Aquarium area is the practical substitute.

  • The Pudong-side wharf at the Binjiang Avenue gives a cleaner skyline view than the Bund-side end, because the Customs House does not block it.
  • Shoot from the ferry at either end of its route when the two banks overlap in the frame.
  • Long exposures of the moving ferries and barge lights work from the elevated promenade at the north Bund.

Lens choices for Shanghai

Shanghai rewards two different tools. For the Bund and Lujiazui, a 70 to 200 mm lens lets you compress the skyline and separate the towers from the buildings in front of them. For the French Concession lanes and the water town canals, a 24 to 35 mm wide angle is what makes the small space feel deep, particularly in Zhujiajiao where the lane is barely two metres across.

Glass and reflections are the real difficulty in the Lujiazui area. A circular polariser will let you shoot through the glass roof of the Pudong promenade without the reflected sky washing out the towers, and it also removes the surface glare off the Huangpu when you are working near the water.

For the night frames, the city is bright enough that a tripod is optional rather than essential on the Bund, but it becomes necessary on the quieter canal bridges and inside the Shanghai Museum galleries, where the light is deliberately kept low.

Frequently Asked Questions: Booking in Shanghai

Where is the single best viewpoint for the Shanghai skyline?

It depends on which half of the story you want. For the classic shot of the three towers, the north end of the Bund promenade near the Waibaidu Bridge works, with a long lens. For the Bund buildings reflected in the foreground, shoot from the Pudong side at Binjiang Avenue. For a raised position over the river itself, the public ferry between the Bund and South Pudong costs only a few yuan and puts you on the water in the middle of the composition.

When is the best time to photograph the French Concession plane trees?

The last two weeks of November, when the leaves turn from green to gold and the low autumn sun comes through them from the west. Xuhui district stopped clearing the leaf litter along the Wukang Road and Anfu Road blocks from 2013, so the ground itself becomes part of the image. Arrive before 8 am or after 5 pm, because the leaf-viewing crowds concentrate in the middle of the day.

Do I need a reservation for the Shanghai Museum?

The permanent galleries have been free since March 2008 but do require a reservation, released through the museum's official WeChat account and its website, and it is worth booking a few days ahead in peak season. Large travelling exhibitions are separately ticketed and separately booked, and the 'Pyramid Peak' ancient Egyptian exhibition that ran in 2024 and 2025 required advance allocation for the same reason.

Is it worth shooting the Bund at night as well as at sunset?

Yes, and they are different pictures. Sunset gives you warm light raking across the stone facades with a blue sky above the towers. After dark the illumination switches on and the priority becomes reflections in the Huangpu and the lit facades, which is a better subject for a long exposure from the Pudong side, where you can include the moving ferry lights in the water.

How do I get good water town photographs without a car getting stuck?

Park outside the old town, because the lanes in Zhujiajiao and Fengjing are narrow and closed to traffic at busy times, and walk in. The best light in the water towns is either very early, before the coaches arrive and the lane is still empty, or late afternoon, when the low sun comes down the canal and lights the far end of the street. Bring a wide angle and shoot from the bridge looking along the canal, not across it.

What about drone photography in Shanghai?

Drone flights are restricted in the dense central districts and around the Bund in particular, and the waterfront has security restrictions. The outlying areas, the water towns and Chongming, are more practical, but you still need to check local restrictions and stay well clear of airports and controlled airspace. A high vantage from a building terrace in the French Concession often beats what a drone would have given you anyway.
