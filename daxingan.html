Name Origin

The name Greater Khingan refers to the range itself — the Greater Khingan Mountains, whose name comes from the Manchu word for a high ridge. The range was the boundary between the forests of the east and the grasslands of the west, and along it ran the old post road from Mergen to Mohe, the courier route that connected the Qing court with the far north.

Education & Universities

Higher education is served by the Daxinganling campus of Inner Mongolia University for Nationalities and local vocational colleges, with programmes in forestry, teacher training, cold-region agriculture and timber industry management.

Getting Around

Getting around the Greater Khingan is by road and rail along the foot of the range. The Bei-China railway runs from Jiagedaqi to Mohe along the western side of the mountains, the G111 national highway follows the same line, and the airports at Jiagedaqi and Mohe handle the rest. Distances are long and the forest roads are slow, so plan for driving rather than for touring the towns.

Natural Resources & Environment

The prefecture is 82,929 km² of forest and mountain — 64,768 km² in Heilongjiang and 18,160 km² in Inner Mongolia — and it is one of the largest intact boreal forest regions left in the world. It sits in the cold temperate zone, with a long severe winter, and its rivers drain north through the Nen and Gan rivers. The protected areas include six national nature reserves and three national forest parks.

Food & Shopping in Greater Khingan

What to eat and what to bring home from Greater Khingan

🍽️ Must-Try Greater Khingan Cuisine

The Greater Khingan's table is forest food: cold-water fish from the Nen and Shen rivers, wild mushrooms in season, home-smoked meat in dumplings, and the berries of the northern forests — the Arctic blueberry is a Chinese geographical indication product in its own right.

🛍️ Shopping in Greater Khingan

The county towns sell wild mushrooms, dried berries, birch sap products, forest honey, carved wooden items and the reindeer products of the Ewenki villages in the far north.

🍵 Tea & Drinks

Tea is not a forest tradition, but it is drunk hard and hot in the long winters — a strong black tea with sugar, and ginger tea with honey in the timber towns when the temperature drops below -30 °C.

🏛️ Heritage & Landmarks

The heritage here is the forest and the north rather than the monument. The old Qing post road from Mergen to Mohe is a national heritage site along with the Li Jinyong shrine, and the Ewenki reindeer herders of the northern villages keep a hunting culture that is unique in China. The Soviet-era timber town architecture of Jiagedaqi, the Orthodox church at the old Russian settlements, and the story of the railway that built the range's towns are the modern half of the story.

Best Time to Visit Greater Khingan

June to August for the forest and the long days; late August and March for the aurora; September for the autumn colour; January to February for the deep snow.

🌸 Spring

Chinese spring · April–May — the thaw, the mud season on the forest roads and the first green in the birch. (daytime -0.4–18.9°C, nights around -16.1°C; about 21 mm of precipitation a month)

☀️ Summer

Chinese summer · June–August — long daylight, mosquitoes in the wetlands, wildflowers and the best driving conditions; late August brings the first aurora season. (daytime 23.9–25.7°C, nights around 9.6°C; about 126 mm of precipitation a month)

🍂 Autumn

Chinese autumn · September–October — the birch and larch turn gold and red across the range, with crisp clear air. (daytime -5.3–17.9°C, nights around -18.2°C; about 28 mm of precipitation a month)

❄️ Winter

Chinese winter · November–March — deep snow, -30 °C or colder, rime on the trees and the famous ice lantern festival in Mohe. (daytime -14.6–-8.6°C, nights around -28.4°C; about 5 mm of precipitation a month)

Tea Culture & Teahouse Guide in Greater Khingan

What to drink in Greater Khingan, where to drink it, and how the locals do it.

🍵 Tea Houses of Greater Khingan

Tea is not a forest tradition, but it is drunk hard and hot in the long winters — a strong black tea with sugar, and ginger tea with honey in the timber towns when the temperature drops below -30 °C.

🫖 A Gongfu Tasting

Ask your driver to stop at a house where the tea is brewed properly: a small pot, several cups, and a host who will explain how the leaves change as the water cools. It is the cheapest and most relaxing hour of the day.

💡 Tea Etiquette Tips

Pour for others before yourself, and pour with the other hand as a small sign of respect. Tapping two fingers on the table is the traditional way to say thank you.

Most houses will refill your covered bowl for free — there is no need to order again.

Tea Culture & Teahouse Guide in Greater Khingan

Where to sit down for a bowl of tea in Greater Khingan

🍵 Tea Houses of Greater Khingan

Tea culture runs through the day in Greater Khingan: loose-leaf houses serve the region's own greens in covered bowls, and the older teahouses around the old town are where the city's social life happens.

🫖 A Gongfu Tasting

Most visitors try a gongfu-style tasting session — a host brews one tea at a time and explains how the leaves open — and pair it with the local snacks sold in the same establishments.

💡 Tea Etiquette Tips

Ask your driver for the teahouse that locals favour rather than the loudest one on the tourist route; the best rooms are usually a short walk from the market, and a quiet hour there is the cheapest and most rewarding way to understand the city's pace.
