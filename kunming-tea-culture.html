Overview: Tea Culture in Kunming

Kunming is the trading floor for Yunnan tea: pu-erh pressed into 357-gram cakes and Dianhong, the province's black tea developed in 1939, both move through the city's wholesale tea markets. The mild highland climate - the city sits at about 1,900 metres - doubles as natural storage for aging raw pu-erh, which is why collectors keep warehouses here.

The tea

Pu-erh (普洱茶)

Pu-erh is a fermented tea from Yunnan, sold as pressed cakes or bricks. Raw (sheng) tea is sun-dried and aged for years, while ripe (shu) tea is piled and turned over weeks of wet fermentation - a technique developed at Kunming's tea factory in 1973 - producing the deep colour and earthy taste that aged cakes are prized for. The standard round cake weighs 357 grams.

How to have it here: Kunming's tea markets pour both styles side by side: boiling water, one rinse, then ten or more short rounds gongfu-style. Cakes, bowl-shaped tuocha and bricks are sold by mountain and by year - Yiwu, Menghai and Lincang are the origin names to read - and spring-harvest cakes carry the clearest premium.

Dianhong (滇红)

Dianhong is Yunnan's black tea, made from the large-leaf buds of the region's tea trees and fully oxidised - the standard for Yunnan black tea since it was developed in the 1930s. The first 15 tonnes came out of the Shunning works (today's Fengqing) in 1939 and were shipped to Britain through Hong Kong in the same year.

How to have it here: In Kunming it is the easy daily brew: 90-95°C water, three to four minutes, drunk plain or with milk in the Hong Kong style. Higher grades show golden tips in the leaf, and Fengqing and Lincang are the origin labels worth reading on the pack.

Tea etiquette in Kunming

Pu-erh is brewed hard: 100°C water, the first one or two pours discarded as a rinse, then infusions of 10-20 seconds stretched as the rounds climb - a decent cake holds ten to fifteen. The small cups are drained in one go, and the host keeps count, announcing the round so nobody pours a spent leaf twice.

Buying carries its own manners: tasting before paying is expected at Kunming's tea markets, and a host will pour several candidate cakes in sequence for comparison. Turning a cake over to check the press date and the inner ticket pressed into the leaf is normal diligence, not rudeness, and bargaining at market stalls is part of the posted game.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between sheng and shu pu-erh?

Sheng (raw) is the old way: sun-dried leaf pressed into 357-gram cakes and aged for years to mellow. Shu (ripe) is the shortcut developed at Kunming's tea factory in 1973 - the leaf is piled and turned through weeks of wet fermentation to reach in months a depth that raw tea needs a decade for. Both are sold by year and by mountain of origin.

Why do collectors store cakes in Kunming?

The city sits at about 1,900 metres with mild temperatures year round and a stable humidity pattern, conditions that let raw cakes age slowly without mould. Dealers therefore keep warehouses here, and market vendors will quote a cake's press year - sometimes 10, 20 or more years back - as the main pricing fact.

How do I brew pu-erh correctly?

Boiling water, about 100°C, is the rule. Rinse the leaf once or twice first, then pour short rounds of 10-20 seconds, stretching the time as the rounds climb; a good cake supports ten to fifteen rounds. One portion of leaf in a small pot serves a table, which is why the pour sizes stay small.

What is Dianhong and how is it drunk?

Yunnan's black tea, fully oxidised and made from large-leaf buds; the first 15 tonnes were produced at Fengqing in 1939 and exported to Britain the same year. Brew it at 90-95°C for three to four minutes, plain or with milk. Grades with a high share of golden tips sit at the top of the price sheet.
