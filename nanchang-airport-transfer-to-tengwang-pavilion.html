The Pavilion of Prince Teng stands on the east bank of the Gan River in north-west Nanchang, first built in 653 AD as the residence of Li Yuanying, Prince of Teng. Rebuilt 29 times across thirteen centuries, the present reinforced-concrete structure in Song-dynasty style was completed on 8 October 1989 from a plan by the architect Liang Sicheng. It ranks among the Three Great Towers of southern China together with Yueyang Tower and the Yellow Crane Pavilion. Changbei Airport lies 28 kilometres north, roughly a 45-minute drive.

Nanchang Changbei International Airport (KHN)

28 km from the Nanchang terminal, about 45 minutes by car.

Changbei sits 28 kilometres north of the city and opened to traffic on 10 September 1999, replacing the old Xiangtang field. The road into town follows the airport expressway south to the Gan River, crosses by the Bayi Bridge, and reaches the pavilion's platform on the east bank in the old north-west quarter of the city.

Why transfer to the Pavilion of Prince Teng?

The building owes its standing to a single text. In 675 the poet Wang Bo attended a gathering here and wrote the Preface to the Pavilion of Prince Teng, whose line about a lone goose rising with the sunset is still quoted across the Chinese-speaking world. A stainless steel tablet at the entrance carries Mao Zedong's calligraphy of that couplet, and interior displays reconstruct the instruments and music of the period the preface describes.

The numbers behind the rebuild reward the trip on their own. The main structure rises 57.5 metres over nine storeys with 13,000 square metres of floor area, set on a 12-metre concrete platform that stands for the vanished city wall. When the Forbidden City was laid out, its corner towers copied the pavilion as it appeared in Song paintings. Two flanking structures, the Pressing River Pavilion and the Pulling Emerald-Green Pavilion, were restored beside the main hall in 1991, and the surrounding streets have grown into the centre of Nanchang's antiques trade.

  • Present building completed 8 October 1989 to Liang Sicheng's design
  • Nine storeys, 57.5 metres tall, 13,000 square metres of floor space
  • One of the Three Great Towers of southern China with Yueyang and Yellow Crane
  • Corner towers of the Forbidden City imitate its Song-dynasty form

What to do at the Pavilion of Prince Teng once you arrive

The visit runs floor by floor rather than as a single lookout. Each of the nine levels holds a themed hall: reconstructed Tang and Song instruments, murals from the preface's imagery, and a theatre stage where period music is performed on fixed schedules. The upper galleries open onto the Gan River to the west, and the view downstream toward the Bayi Bridge is the one the 675 text describes, framed between the restored corner pavilions of the 1991 works.

The base platform doubles as an exhibition of the site's own history, with scale models of the Tang, Song, Yuan, Ming and Qing versions of the pavilion, each differing in shape and height. Allow sixty to ninety minutes for the interior at an unhurried pace. The riverside promenade in front is free to walk, and the antique-market streets behind the complex open through the day for browsing after the descent.

  • The final gallery floor is the least crowded; performances fill the theatre floor first
  • Combine the visit with the August 1 Uprising sites nearby in the same district
  • Late afternoon light falls on the river side, which is the side the main murals face

Arriving at Nanchang Changbei International Airport

Changbei was built between October 1996 and September 1999 and originally designed for two million passengers a year; after an expansion completed in 2011 it recorded 11.9 million in a recent twelve-month count, with 92,149 aircraft movements. It remains a compact single-terminal field, so walking distances from gate to kerb are short by big-airport standards. Twenty minutes covers baggage claim and domestic arrivals formalities in normal conditions.

Your driver waits in the arrivals hall with a name sign and follows the flight number, so schedule slippage adjusts itself without a call. The expressway run south is the straightforward part; the variable stretch is the river crossing, where the Bayi Bridge thickens at the evening peak and the Honggu tunnel is the usual alternative. Landing at midday, the whole transfer sits close to the 45-minute mark.

What the transfer costs

Sedan service between Changbei and the pavilion district is normally billed between 90 and 140 yuan including fuel and the expressway toll, with the car class rather than the meter deciding the figure. An MPV runs 130 to 180 yuan and carries five or six with luggage. These are point-to-point rates; the driver waits at the arrivals kerb rather than charging hourly.

Where a transfer stretches into a wait-and-return or a full day around Nanchang's sites, pricing converts to a package quoted by the half or whole day. Complimentary waiting at the terminal usually spans an hour, which absorbs moderate delays. Metered taxis from the rank work out cheaper but queue at peak banks of arrivals, and the rank is the one place you cannot pre-book a named vehicle.

The drop-off point is the pavilion's north gate square on Yanjiang Road; coaches and private cars share the lay-by there. Have the driver aim for the north gate rather than the riverside promenade, which is closed to traffic for most of its length.

Frequently Asked Questions

How far is Changbei Airport from the Pavilion of Prince Teng?

The airport is 28 kilometres north of central Nanchang and the pavilion stands in the north-west of the old city on the Gan River, so the drive is close to 30 kilometres by road. With the expressway and the Bayi Bridge flowing, it takes about 45 minutes; the bridge approach can add 15 to 20 minutes in the evening peak.

Which metro line serves the pavilion?

Line 1 of the Nanchang Metro calls at Tengwangge station, a few hundred metres south of the entrance plaza. The line runs east-west through the old city, so it also links the pavilion with the Bayi Square and the railway stations. From the airport there is no rail link, which is why most visitors use a car for the first leg.

Is the current building the original one?

No. The pavilion of 653 AD was destroyed and rebuilt 29 times, most recently burned by retreating defenders in October 1926. The present structure dates from 8 October 1989, built in reinforced concrete to a Song-dynasty design prepared by Liang Sicheng, standing 57.5 metres over nine storeys on a 12-metre platform.

What is the Preface to the Pavilion of Prince Teng?

It is a piece of parallel prose written at a banquet here in 675 by Wang Bo, then in his twenties. Its most famous couplet describes a lone goose flying against the sunset with the autumn river and sky a single colour, and the text made the building a household name. Mao Zedong's brush rendering of the couplet hangs at the entrance on a steel tablet.

How long should I allow for a visit?

Sixty to ninety minutes covers the nine floors at a reasonable pace, longer if you time a music performance in the theatre hall. The dynastic model displays on the platform level repay ten minutes on their own. Visitors pairing the pavilion with the riverside promenade and the antique streets behind it usually budget half a day.
