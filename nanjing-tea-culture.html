Overview: Tea Culture in Nanjing

Nanjing's own tea is Yuhua, a needle-shaped green tea named for the Yuhuatai terrace south of the old city, with records placing its origin in the late Qing county of Jiangning. The city's tea science goes back further in institutions: in 1907 the Jiangnan Tea Planting Bureau, China's first state-run tea research body, opened near Purple Mountain, and its 47 mu of surviving gardens were replanted in the 1950s.

The tea in Nanjing

Yuhua (雨花茶)

Yuhua is a green tea from Nanjing, named for the way the dried needles resemble falling rain. It is one of the three teas traditionally associated with the city. The finished leaf is a straight needle no more than 3 cm long, made from a single bud with one leaf picked around the Qingming festival, with the ratio of bud to leaf between 1:3 and 2:3. Processing runs through fixation, rolling, and shaping into needles.

How to have it here: Under national standard GB/T 20605-2006, only tea from eight areas of Nanjing may be called Yuhua: the Zhongshan Mausoleum garden, the Yuhuatai Martyrs' Cemetery, and the districts of Jiangning, Lishui, Gaochun, Pukou, Luhe, Yuhuatai and Qixia. The tea was first fired on 20 April 1959, and the making technique was entered on the national intangible cultural heritage list in 2021 as item VIII-148.

Jasmine tea (茉莉花茶)

Jasmine in this case is scented into the leaf rather than mixed into it: green tea is layered with fresh blossoms over several nights, and the gathering is done in the evening, at the point where the flower gives off the most. Nanjing's connection to the plant is old, since Zhang Dai's late-Ming account of the Qinhuai river houses describes the scent rising from the waterside buildings on both banks of an inner river 4.8 kilometres long.

How to have it here: Jasmine is the scent that goes with the city's river-house and teahouse trade, where a pot of scented green is the accompaniment to a table of small dishes. The same scenting craft works on any green base, so a Longjing scented with jasmine is called Longjing jasmine, and Suzhou, to the west, once grew the finest jasmine tea in China from the 12th century onward, though its elaborate method and the strict quality it demanded on the flowers made it costly.

Teahouses in Nanjing

Old Teahouse exhibition, Nanjing Museum (南京博物院老茶馆)

One of the permanent displays in the Nanjing Museum is an Old Teahouse, presented alongside the Jiangsu intangible cultural heritage exhibition, the masters' workshop and a small theatre. The museum occupies more than 70,000 square metres in the former Banshan Garden inside Zhongshanmen, and holds over 430,000 objects; its history gallery occupies the site of the National Central Museum, whose preparatory office was set up in 1934.

Area: Zhongshanmennei, south foot of Zijin Mountain, Nanjing

Jiangsu Tea Museum garden (江苏省茶博园)

A provincial tea museum garden at the northern foot of Maoshan in Jurong, a county-level city of 1,377.86 square kilometres that has long stood as Nanjing's southeastern gateway and was for centuries administered from the city. It is named by the China Tea Society as a base for tea science popularisation and education, and its plantings and displays cover the picking and firing of the Jiangsu tea types, Yuhua among them.

Area: Jurong, at the northern foot of Maoshan, southeast of Nanjing

How Nanjing serves Yuhua

Yuhua is poured in a glass or a lidded gaiwan, and the way it behaves in the vessel is the point: the straight needles stand upright on the first steep and settle to the bottom as they soften, so a full glass shows how far the infusion has run. A light hand is enough. Use about 3 grams to 150 ml of water at roughly 80 degrees, and count the first steep at one minute, adding a few seconds each round after that, since a needle leaf gives up its substance in small instalments.

The name sets a rule about place that people here take seriously. Because the 2006 standard fixes the Yuhua name to eight named areas of the city, a label that gives no district is not describing protected tea, and a shop that cannot say whether its leaf comes from the Yuhuatai, Zhongshan Mausoleum or Qixia side has not done the work that the designation asks for. Ask for the district rather than for a brand name.

The city also keeps its tea in museums, and visiting one is a reasonable way to understand the grades before buying. The Nanjing Museum's Old Teahouse display sits with the Jiangsu intangible heritage exhibition in a complex of more than 70,000 square metres, and the provincial tea museum garden at the foot of Maoshan covers the picking and firing methods in planting. Both are places where a visitor can see the whole sequence from bud to needle rather than a single finished tin.

Frequently Asked Questions: Booking in Nanjing

How did a tea get named after a place where martyrs are buried?

Yuhua takes its name from the Yuhuatai terrace in the south of the city, which in 1958 was made the site of a national martyrs' cemetery. The year the tea was created, the Jiangsu provincial committee for new speciality teas fixed the leaf shape as a pine needle and named the tea after the falling-rain terraces and the falling-rain needles alike, so that both the place and the leaf carried the same idea of a tribute. The first batch was fired on 20 April 1959.

What did the Jiangnan Tea Planting Bureau do?

Founded in 1907 near Purple Mountain by the Jiangsu circuit official Zheng Shihuang, it was a state-run institution for tea trials and production and is counted as China's first official tea research body, treated as a starting point for modern Chinese tea science. Tea gardens expanded through the Republic of China period, and after 1949 the local government replanted and maintained the 47 mu of gardens left over from that era in the 1950s.

Which parts of Nanjing can grow Yuhua?

Eight areas are named in the national standard GB/T 20605-2006, published on 3 November 2006: the Zhongshan Mausoleum garden, the Yuhuatai Martyrs' Cemetery, and the districts of Jiangning, Lishui, Gaochun, Pukou, Luhe, Yuhuatai and Qixia. The tea also became a protected geographical indication product in 2004, and the production technique was listed as a national intangible cultural heritage item in 2021.

How is the leaf picked and what does a good one look like?

Quality Yuhua uses an opening bud with one leaf picked around Qingming, and rejects hollow buds, insect-damaged buds and buds picked after rain. The bud-and-leaf length must not exceed 3 cm, the ratio of bud to leaf sits between 1:3 and 2:3, and the angle between them is no more than 45 degrees. The stages are fixation, rolling, shaping and drying, followed by sifting and baking of the finished leaf.

How much of Yuhua is made by machine today?

By 2010 about 90 per cent of Yuhua production used modern tea machinery rather than the traditional hand process, a shift driven by the arrival of mechanical equipment through the 1950s and after. Hand-made Yuhua therefore survives as a specialist craft and as a demonstration subject in museums and demonstration gardens, and the intangible heritage listing of 2021 covers the traditional technique rather than the machine output.

Where can a visitor in Nanjing see tea being processed?

The Nanjing Museum in the former Banshan Garden inside Zhongshanmen holds the Old Teahouse display along with the Jiangsu intangible cultural heritage exhibition, in a site of more than 70,000 square metres with over 430,000 objects in the collection. The provincial tea museum garden at the northern foot of Maoshan in Jurong, on Nanjing's southeastern approach, was designated by the China Tea Society as a base for tea science education and covers the picking and firing of Jiangsu teas.
