Why Beijing is a Tea Lover's Destination

Beijing may be famous for its imperial palaces and the Great Wall, but the city's tea culture is equally remarkable. As the capital of multiple Chinese dynasties, Beijing became the nation's tea capital — a place where tea traditions from every province converged, evolved, and were refined.

Unlike the tea gardens of Hangzhou or the tea markets of Yunnan, Beijing's tea culture is urban, social, and deeply intertwined with daily life. You'll find teahouses in every hutong, from humble street-side establishments serving plain chrysanthemum tea to elegant venues where masters perform traditional tea ceremonies.

The most distinctive feature of Beijing tea culture is its inclusion of snacks. Unlike tea houses in southern China that serve tea alone, Beijing teahouses are social spaces where friends gather over endless pots of pu-erh accompanied by sunflower seeds, candied fruit, and traditional pastries.

The Rich History of Tea in Beijing

How China's capital became the heart of the nation's tea culture.

🚢 The Tea Road to Beijing

Since the Yuan Dynasty (1271-1368), Beijing served as the terminus of the Grand Canal — the massive waterway that connected China's rice and tea-growing south to the northern capital. Tea from Fujian, Zhejiang, and Yunnan traveled hundreds of miles by barge to reach Beijing's imperial court.

The Ming Dynasty (1368-1644) cemented tea's role in Beijing's social fabric. Emperors held elaborate tea ceremonies at the Temple of Heaven, and the practice of offering tea to guests became a cornerstone of Chinese hospitality etiquette that persists today.

🏛️ Tea and the Imperial Court

During the Qing Dynasty, the imperial court developed elaborate tea rituals. The Emperor would taste new tea harvests before anyone else, and the finest teas were reserved exclusively for the Forbidden City. Commoners could only access lower-grade teas — a distinction that gave certain teas an almost mythological status.

☕ From Palace to People

After the fall of the Qing Dynasty in 1912, tea culture democratized. Teahouses proliferated throughout Beijing's hutongs, becoming the city's primary social spaces. Intellectuals debated politics, merchants conducted business, and neighbors caught up on gossip — all over pots of shared tea.

This tradition continues today. Beijing's teahouses are living museums, where you can experience the same unhurried social ritual that has connected Beijingers for centuries. The sound of tea being poured, the crack of sunflower seeds being cracked, and the murmur of conversation create an atmosphere unlike anywhere else in China.

🎭 Tea and Performance

Beijing is the only city in China where tea houses developed a strong tradition of live performance. Traditional teahouses near Qianmen offer nightly shows featuring Peking opera, acrobatics, and the legendary face-changing technique. This marriage of tea and performance makes Beijing's teahouses uniquely cultural destinations.

Must-Try Teas in Beijing

The teas that define Beijing's tea culture, from aged pu-erh to fragrant jasmine.

🫖

Pu-erh Tea (普洱茶)

Beijing's #1 tea

The dark, aged tea that defines Beijing. Fermented for years or even decades, pu-erh has an earthy, smooth flavor that locals believe aids digestion — especially helpful after the rich food of northern Chinese cuisine. Aged pu-erh (老普洱) can cost hundreds of dollars per饼 (cake).

Try at: Lao She Tea House, street-side teahouses

🌸

Jasmine Tea (茉莉花茶)

The fragrance of Beijing

Beijing's signature scented tea. Jasmine flowers are layered with green tea leaves, allowing the fragrance to infuse over multiple scenting cycles. Tongrentang (同仁堂) and Zhang Yuxuan (张一元), both historic pharmacies-tea shops near Qianmen, are famous for their jasmine blends.

Try at: Historic tea shops on Dashilan

🌼

Chrysanthemum Tea (菊花茶)

Health & wellness

Dried chrysanthemum flowers steeped in hot water — a staple in Beijing homes and teahouses alike. Beloved for its cooling properties and mild sweetness. Often combined with goji berries for a health-boosting brew. Perfect for dry Beijing winters.

Try at: Any teahouse, homes

🍃

Longjing (Dragon Well)

China's most famous green tea

While grown near Hangzhou, Longjing is widely enjoyed in Beijing's teahouses. The flat, sword-shaped leaves produce a sweet, nutty brew with hints of chestnut. Served in glass cups so you can admire the beautiful leaves.

Try at: Lao She Tea House, upscale teahouses

🍂

Lotus Leaf Tea (荷叶茶)

Refreshing summer brew

Dried lotus leaves steeped to create a light, refreshing drink. Popular in summer for its cooling properties and subtle herbal flavor. Often combined with rice or corn. A traditional Beijing summer refreshment.

Try at: Summer teahouses, Yuyuan area

🫘

Red Bean Tea (红豆茶)

Sweet treat

Sweet red bean soup served as a tea accompaniment — a uniquely Beijing treat. The warm, sweet red beans provide a perfect contrast to bitter pu-erh. Usually offered free as a snack with tea orders at traditional teahouses.

Try at: Lao She Tea House, historic teahouses

Best Tea Houses in Beijing

From iconic institutions to hidden gems — where to experience authentic Beijing tea culture.

🏛️ Iconic Tea Houses

Lao She Tea House (老舍茶馆)

Most Famous

老舍茶馆 · Near Qianmen, Dongcheng

Beijing's most iconic teahouse since 1989. Five floors of traditional decor, authentic Beijing-style dim sum, and nightly performances of Peking opera, acrobatics, and folk art. Visited by heads of state. The second-floor evening shows (7:30-9:30pm) are the highlight.

🕐 Daily 10:00-21:30
🎭 Evening shows from ¥180
📍 3 Qianmen East Street
💰 Tea from ¥50/person

Gardens of the Five Dragons (五龙亭茶馆)

五龙亭 · Jingshan Park

Located in Jingshan Park directly north of the Forbidden City. This series of pavilions overlooking the palace offers tea with a view — sip pu-erh while gazing at the rooftops of the Forbidden City. A uniquely Beijing experience that combines tea culture with imperial scenery.

🕐 6:00-20:00
🎟️ Park entry ¥2
📍 Jingshan Park
💰 Tea from ¥30/cup

🏮 Historic Tea Streets

Dashilan Tea Street (大栅栏茶街)

大栅栏 · Xicheng District

The historic Dashilan area near Qianmen is Beijing's most concentrated tea district. Traditional establishments like Tongrentang (同仁堂, founded 1669) and Zhang Yuxuan (张一元, founded 1900) have sold premium teas here for over a century. The street comes alive in afternoon when locals gather for tea and conversation.

Visit Tongrentang for medicinal herbal teas and Zhang Yuxuan for premium jasmine blends. Both shops offer tea tastings and can ship purchases worldwide.

Luoyang Lane Tea House (洛阳茶馆)

洛阳茶馆 · Near Beixinqiao

A hidden gem in a quiet hutong near Beixinqiao. This modest establishment has been serving tea since the 1950s and attracts a loyal local clientele. No performances, no tourist crowds — just authentic tea culture in a laid-back atmosphere. The owner is a wealth of tea knowledge and loves to share.

Try their aged pu-erh collection — some cakes are 20+ years old.

☕ Local Favorites

Guangfu Tea House (广福茶馆)

Hutong gem · Wangfujing area

Small, family-run teahouse popular with locals. Excellent selection of pu-erh at reasonable prices. No English menu, but pointing and smiling works. Great for an authentic, unfussy experience.

Yuemin Tea House (悦茗茶馆)

Hutong gem · Guloudajie

Converted courtyard house near the Drum Tower. Serves premium Longjing and Biluochun. The courtyard seating is magical in autumn. Offers tea ceremony experiences by appointment.

Wang's Tea House (王记茶馆)

Hutong gem · Dongzhimen

A neighborhood institution with zero tourists. Simple plastic stools, stainless steel thermoses of tea, and sunflower seeds. This is how Beijingers actually drink tea — casual, social, and deliciously unpretentious.

Beijing Tea Etiquette — How to Drink Like a Local

The unwritten rules of Beijing teahouses, from pouring tea to cracking seeds.

🍵 The Pouring Ritual

  • Never fill a cup completely — leave space for the aroma
  • Tap two fingers on the table to thank the pourer (a sign of respect)
  • Always pour tea for others before yourself
  • Hold the lid while pouring to prevent dripping
  • Refill before the cup goes dry — an empty cup is considered rude

🥢 The Snacks

  • Sunflower seeds (瓜子) — the classic accompaniment, cracked one by one
  • Candied hawthorn (糖葫芦) — sweet-sour, on a stick
  • Preserved plums (话梅) — sour and salty
  • Red bean soup (红豆汤) — sweet, often free
  • Tea eggs (茶叶蛋) — hard-boiled eggs simmered in tea and soy

💰 Pricing & Tipping

Beijing teahouses typically charge per pot of tea (¥30-200) rather than per cup. Snacks are priced separately. Tipping is not expected but appreciated at upscale venues — ¥10-20 is generous.

Budget teahouse: ¥30-60/pot + ¥20-30 snacks

Mid-range: ¥80-150/pot + ¥50 snacks

Upscale (with show): ¥180-400/person including tea + dim sum + show

💡 Pro Tips

  • Visit Lao She Tea House for the full experience with a show — book ahead
  • Go to Dashilan in the afternoon for the most atmosphere
  • Ask for "old pu-erh" (老普洱) if you want the good stuff
  • Bring cash — many historic teahouses don't accept cards
  • Most teahouses allow you to stay for hours — no rush
