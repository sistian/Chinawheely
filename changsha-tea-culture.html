Overview: Tea Culture in Changsha

Changsha sits at the meeting point of two tea trades. It is the wholesale capital of the dark teas pressed in the mountains of western Hunan, and by the late Qing it was named among China's four great tea markets, alongside three other urban centres, because the tea of the hills north and west of the city was carried out through its river port. At the same time the city's own cup has always been the pale yellow of Junshan Yinzhen, the yellow tea made from single unopened buds on an island in Dongting Lake about 130 km north at Yueyang. That tea now carries two official distinctions in twenty years: the making technique was listed as the fifth batch of national intangible cultural heritage in 2021, and in 2022 the technique travelled with Chinese traditional tea-making practices onto the UNESCO list of the intangible cultural heritage of humanity, the only yellow tea to be so included.

The tea in Changsha

Junshan Yinzhen (君山银针)

Junshan Yinzhen is a yellow tea from an island in Dongting Lake in Hunan, made from single unopened buds. It is gently fixed and dried, which keeps the leaf straight and the taste light. The island of Junshan lies in Dongting Lake at Yueyang, roughly 130 km north of Changsha, so the tea reaches the provincial capital as a finished product of a lake island rather than a hillside garden. The making technique was added in 2021 to the fifth batch of national intangible cultural heritage, and in 2022 it was inscribed with Chinese traditional tea-making techniques and their related practices on the UNESCO list of the intangible cultural heritage of humanity, the only yellow tea among them.

How to have it here: In Changsha it is brewed as a delicate green-looking infusion in a glass or a white porcelain cup, with water at about 85°C and a steep of 60 to 90 seconds, and the leaf is generally left in the vessel so the colour can be watched as it fades. It is served in the city as a gift-grade tea and in the teahouses of the old commercial streets as the item to compare against the local dark tea. Buds only, no leaf, is the mark of the real thing; a price list that says Silver Needle grade is quoting a different tea altogether.

Dark tea (heicha) (黑茶)

Heicha, or dark tea, is post-fermented tea from Hunan and Anhui. The best known examples are Anhua dark tea and the pressed Fu brick tea of Shaanxi; both are stacked and covered during a long post-fermentation that gives them their dark colour and mellow taste. The Anhua hills lie in the tea country west of Changsha, and the brick form of this tea was built for transport northward towards Tibet and the northern markets, which is the trade that made Changsha one of the 4 great tea markets of China by the late Qing, in 1911.

How to have it here: Bricks and bundles are the retail unit in the city: a piece is broken off, dropped into a pot or a covered bowl, and brewed hard at near-boiling water for 2-3 minutes, sometimes with a second boil over the same leaf. A 5 g piece is the usual measure. The pressed brick travels well and keeps for years, so it is the tea bought by weight and stored at home, while the loose dark tea is drunk from a bowl in the tea shops of the old market streets. It is the everyday brew of Hunan, darker and heavier than the yellow tea of the lake.

Brick tea (砖茶)

The compressed form of dark tea, the brick, is pressed for transport rather than for brewing in the field, and it is the traditional brew of northern China and Inner Mongolia. Changsha's role was at the southern end of that road, where the pressed bricks of the Hunan hills were consigned to the caravan stations and to the Tibetan and Mongolian markets beyond. The block, not the loose leaf, was the shipping form that made a long journey possible, and it is the form still found in the tea shops around the old wharves, where by the late Qing, in 1911, the city was one of the 4 great tea markets of China.

How to have it here: A whole brick is bought to keep; a piece of about 10 grams goes into a pot, is boiled in 500 ml of water, and is then taken in small cups, occasionally with milk, over several sittings. The outer brick is sometimes used to wrap and store other tea, and the older dealers in the city still sell brick by weight, with the year and the mountain of origin written on the wrapping.

Teahouses in Changsha

Fire Palace (火宫殿)

The Fire Palace is one of the long-standing old-brand establishments of Changsha, standing in the Taiping Street quarter of the old city, the same cluster of streets that held the city's tea shops, food stalls and market trade in the late Qing, in 1911, when Changsha was counted among the 4 great tea markets of the country. Its history runs through the twentieth century as the city's best known food house, and the name itself refers to the tiled stove fire of the old kitchens. Tea is part of the same service rather than a separate business: a bowl of Hunan dark tea is drunk with the meal, and the surrounding streets remain the place where the old tea trade of the city is still visible.

Area: Taiping Street area, Changsha

Tea etiquette in Changsha

The Changsha bowl is the dark-tea bowl, and it is drunk hard. A piece of pressed brick or a measure of Anhua dark tea goes into the pot, water comes in at near-boiling, and the first steep stands for two or three minutes rather than the 45 seconds given to scented tea. Refills come from the same leaf and are weaker each time, and the drinker expects to be asked whether another boil is wanted. The yellow tea of Junshan Yinzhen is treated with the opposite care: a glass, water around 85°C, a steep of 60 to 90 seconds, and no leaf removed until the colour has gone.

In the city's tea shops the leaf is judged by hand before it is judged by taste. A buyer opens a brick or a bundle, weighs a small amount, about 5 g, onto the counter, and brews it in the shop's own pot while the seller names the mountain, the year and the grade. Asking to see the pressed inner ticket and the outer wrapping is ordinary practice, not suspicion, and the price quoted is for the tea that has just been brewed. Bulk tea sold by weight is wrapped on the spot, which is why a purchase in Changsha arrives as a package rather than as a tin.

Service in a teahouse follows the Changsha table rather than the individual guest: the server pours for everyone and keeps the pot where all hands can reach it. Cups are filled to about 7 or 8 tenths, never to the brim, and a guest who has finished turns the cup upside down on the tray as the signal that no more is wanted. With 2 teas on the same table, the lighter cup is drunk first and the heavier brew is left for the end of the sitting.

Frequently Asked Questions: Booking in Changsha

What is Junshan Yinzhen and where is it made?

A yellow tea made from single unopened tea buds, grown on Junshan Island in Dongting Lake at Yueyang in Hunan, about 130 km north of Changsha. The buds are gently fixed and dried, which keeps the leaf straight and the colour pale. In 2021 the making technique was listed in the fifth batch of national intangible cultural heritage, and in 2022 it was inscribed with Chinese traditional tea-making techniques on the UNESCO intangible heritage list, the only yellow tea so included.

Why is Changsha called one of China's tea markets?

Because of the trade rather than the growing. By the late Qing, in 1911, the city was counted among the 4 great tea markets of the country, along with 3 other urban centres, on the strength of the tea that came down from the hills of western Hunan and the dark teas pressed in the Anhua area west of the city. The river port carried those teas north, and the compressed brick form was what made the long road to Tibet and Inner Mongolia practical.

How is dark tea brewed, and how is it different from Junshan Yinzhen?

Dark tea is brewed hard: a piece of brick or a measure of Anhua tea, water at near-boiling, and a steep of two or three minutes, with refills over the same leaf. Junshan Yinzhen is the opposite: a glass or white cup, water at about 85°C, a steep of 60 to 90 seconds, and the leaf left in the vessel so the colour can be watched. One is the working brew of Hunan, the other the gift-grade tea of the lake island.

Why are the two UNESCO and national heritage listings important?

Because they place the Junshan technique in a formal register. The 2021 listing was the fifth batch of national intangible cultural heritage for tea-making; the 2022 inscription was as part of Chinese traditional tea-making techniques and their related practices on the UNESCO list of the intangible cultural heritage of humanity. Junshan Yinzhen is the only yellow tea among the inscribed tea practices, which is why it is described as the sole yellow tea on that list.

What should I look for when buying tea in Changsha?

Look at the brick and the wrapping, and ask for the mountain, the year and the grade. A buyer will usually see a small quantity, about 5 g, brewed in the shop before any money is discussed, and the weight quoted is for the leaf that has just been brewed. Loose dark tea is bought by weight and wrapped on the spot, while Junshan Yinzhen should be sold by grade, with buds only and no leaf in the packet.

Where in Changsha is the old tea trade still visible?

In the old commercial quarter around Taiping Street, where the long-standing Fire Palace stands alongside the tea shops and food stalls, and where brick tea and Anhua dark tea are still weighed out, brewed in the shop and wrapped for carrying. The district is also the reason Changsha can still claim the tea-market reputation it held by the late Qing, in 1911, when it was one of the 4 great tea markets of the country.
