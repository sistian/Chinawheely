Overview: Tea Culture in Guangzhou

Guangzhou is the city where tea became a meal. Morning tea - yum cha - begins with a pot chosen at the table and continues through baskets of dim sum carried on trolleys; the Cantonese phrase for the basic order is yi zhong liang jian, 1 pot and 2 baskets. The custom entered the 8th batch of Guangdong's provincial intangible cultural heritage list in 2022, and it rests on a graded ladder of establishments: a cha liao selling 1 li of tea, a cha guan, a cha ju, and the tea house that survives today.

The tea

Gongfu tea (工夫茶)

Gongfu cha is a Cantonese house method rather than a tea in itself: a small pot, a small cup, and short pours repeated many times. It is the way tea has been served across Fujian and Guangdong for generations, and it is why a good pu-erh cake can run to 12 rounds. In a Guangzhou tea house the same discipline governs a plain green tea, where the pot is small enough to hold 4 to 5 pours and the cup is drained rather than filled.

How to have it here: The pot, the lidded brewing vessel and the row of small cups on a wooden tray are set down before the first dish arrives, so the tea runs through the whole sitting. The host pours for the table first and refills cups in turn, and the pot is topped up continuously - a table that runs out of tea is the one failure a tea house cannot allow, so most houses now run 2 or 3 spare kettles.

Pu-erh (普洱茶)

Pu-erh reaches a Guangzhou table as a fermented tea from Yunnan, sold as pressed cakes or bricks. Raw leaf is sun-dried in Yunnan, then heaped and turned for months at a time, which produces the deep colour and earthy taste that aged cakes are prized for. It is the most commonly ordered tea at a Guangzhou table, chosen as much for its effect on a heavy meal of fried and steamed baskets as for its taste, and it reaches the city in cakes and bricks of a standard 357 g.

How to have it here: Cantonese tables treat pu-erh as a workmanlike house tea: a cake or a brick is broken, rinsed with 1 short pour, and then brewed in a small pot through 6 or more rounds. In higher-end restaurants it appears as ju pu, pu-erh scented with chrysanthemum, served alongside Tieguanyin as one of the standard house drinks.

Phoenix Dancong (凤凰单丛)

Phoenix Dancong, also written Phoenix Shancong, is an oolong belonging to the Guangdong family of oolongs. It takes its name from Phoenix Mountain in Phoenix Town, in the northern mountains of Chao'an District, Chaozhou, and the most famous aroma type is the silver-flower Dancong, nicknamed duck-shit Dancong for its smell. In 2004 the Chaozhou municipal government ruled that all packaging and publicity must use the character cong, with the alternative spelling declared a mistake.

How to have it here: Dancong reaches Guangzhou through the Guangdong tea trade and is brewed in the southern style with a small pot, giving a green liquor against a high-rolled leaf. Shops in the city stock it by aroma type and by mountain village, and a guest ordering by name will usually be asked which fragrance they prefer.

Teahouses

Guangzhou Restaurant (广州酒家)

The restaurant traces its origin to the Guangzhou Great Restaurant founded in 1935 and is famous for Cantonese cooking and for its mid-autumn mooncakes, holding the reputation of the first restaurant in the city for food. It took its present name after the Cultural Revolution, was refurbished in July 1983, became the Guangzhou Restaurant Group in 1991, and was listed as a registered protected cultural relic unit of the city in September 2005. It carries the state designation of a time-honoured Chinese brand.

Area: Wenchang South Road at Shangxiajiu Pedestrian Street, Liwan District

Tao Tao Ju (陶陶居)

Tao Tao Ju is a long-established Cantonese tea house on the Shangxiajiu pedestrian street, founded in 1880, the sixth year of the Guangxu reign. The site had been the academy of a wealthy Xiguan family, and the business was later renamed from its earlier name, keeping the words for ease and contentment. Together with Lin Heung Lau it is one of only 2 Guangzhou tea houses still standing that are more than 100 years old, and it is the reference point for the city's morning-tea tradition.

Area: 20 Shifu Road, Xiguan, Liwan District

White Swan Hotel (白天鹅宾馆)

The White Swan is a five-star hotel on Shamian Island funded by the Hong Kong businessman Fok Ying-tung, opened on 6 February 1983 after an agreement signed in April 1979 and a construction start on 19 July 1979. The building covers 110,000 square metres, rises 28 storeys to 102.8 metres, and counted 843 rooms until a 2015 reduction to 520, with 7 restaurants. Its early management practice is often described as a test plot of the reform period, and the ground-floor tea service follows the city's ordinary Cantonese code: the tea is chosen at the table and refilled without being asked.

Area: Southern end of Shamian Island, Liwan District

Fangcun Tea Market (芳村南方茶叶市场)

Fangcun stands on the west bank of the Pearl River in the southwest of the city and has been folded into Liwan District since May 2005. Over the past decade the district's economy has turned to flower, bird, insect and tea and furniture wholesale, and the Nanfang tea market here is the wholesale tea quarter through which Yunnan cakes, Anhui green teas and Guangdong oolongs pass on to retailers. The district's name records a different past: it began as a fishing settlement called huang cun in the early Northern Song and was renamed when flower and fruit planting spread by the late Ming and early Qing.

Area: Fangcun, Liwan District

Table manners in a Guangzhou tea house

The order comes first and the tea is chosen at the same time. A guest who has taken a seat calls for a tea to be opened, and the waiter writes the number of covers on the dim sum card as a tea seat charge. The dishes are chosen from trolleys that circulate with the food, and the guest stamps the card as each basket is taken. Bowls, spoons and teacups are rinsed with boiling water before the first dish lands, and the rinse water is poured into the large bowl the house provides for the purpose. Drinking begins only after the first basket has arrived.

Pouring rules are the part visitors most often get wrong. A diner refills the other cups before their own, filling them about 7 tenths full, and when someone pours for them the diner bends the index and middle finger and taps the table a few times to acknowledge it. To ask for more hot water the guest lifts the pot lid, a practice that dates from the late Qing period, because the lid had once been left closed over a caged bird and a scalded bird had to be paid for. The chargeable wet towels may be declined or returned at payment.

Houses differed by more than decoration. Before 1912 four kinds of premises existed: the cha liao, a bamboo-and-timber shelter by the roadside where a cup of tea cost 1 li; the cha guan, which grew out of the better one-li halls and sat near the docks and the fish, fruit and meat markets, where 2 li bought a pot of poor tea and 2 coarse baskets; the cha ju, a middle-ranking house with better tea and finer pastries; and the tea house proper, which served the Qing bureaucracy at high cost from early morning until night. The Republican period merged the second and third types.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does yi zhong liang jian actually mean?

It is the basic order for morning tea: 1 pot of tea and 2 baskets of dim sum, the 2 baskets traditionally shrimp dumplings and char siu buns, the ones that were most popular when the custom formed. It survives as the standard way of describing a modest tea-house visit, with the number of baskets rising as the meal goes on and with the tea pot refilled throughout.

Which tea is ordered most often in Guangzhou?

Pu-erh, chosen in the tea house for the plain reason that it is said to cut grease after a table of fried and steamed food; jasmine, oolong and green teas are all common as well. In the more expensive restaurants Tieguanyin and ju pu, chrysanthemum-scented pu-erh, are the standard house drinks offered alongside the ordinary selections, and the practice of naming a tea at the moment of seating has not changed in 100 years.

Which old tea houses are still open in the city?

Tao Tao Ju, founded in 1880 at 20 Shifu Road in Xiguan, and Lin Heung Lau, which began as a wedding pastry shop in 1889 and took its present name in 1910, are the 2 Guangzhou tea houses over 100 years old. The Guangzhou Restaurant, founded in 1935 on Wenchang South Road, is the leading name among the restaurant houses that grew out of the same tea-house trade.

How does the guest signal for hot water?

By lifting the lid of the teapot. The custom is said to date from the late Qing period, before 1912, when a customer left the lid closed over a caged parakeet in his cup and the tea boy scalded the bird in pouring, and the house then required every guest to open the lid before refilling. Staff in most houses carry a kettle rather than the traditional shoulder pole, and many now fill from a hot-water machine or a bottled supply.

What did the old Guangdong tea houses cost?

Prices were quoted in li, with 72 li making 1 silver candareen. In the cha liao a cup of tea cost 1 li, giving the nickname one-li hall; in the cha guan, 2 li bought a pot of poor tea with 2 coarse baskets, the two-li hall, enough for a working diner to eat. The cha ju aimed at the middle ranks with better tea and finer pastries, and the old tea house served banquets at a price its Qing official clientele never had to calculate.

Where is the city's wholesale tea trade?

In Fangcun, on the west bank of the Pearl River in the southwest of the city, incorporated into Liwan District in May 2005. Over the last 10 years the district has developed wholesale trade in tea and furniture alongside its flower and bird markets, with the Nanfang tea market as the centre of that trade, where Yunnan cakes and Anhui green teas arrive before being passed on to shops across the city.
