Food & Shopping Guide 🍽 Beijing

Overview: Eating and Shopping in Beijing

Beijing runs two kitchens at once: an imperial one that produced roast duck and court pastries, and a street one built on offal, fermented mung-bean milk and sesame-sauce dips. This page lists seven dishes with verifiable histories and four shopping streets with measured lengths, so you can eat and shop through the capital on facts rather than guesswork.

What to eat

Peking roast duck (北京烤鸭)

A whole-duck banquet dish with two documented schools: hung-oven (roasted over fruitwood in an open hearth) and closed-oven. It evolved from Nanjing roast duck after the Ming moved the capital to Beijing in the 15th century, and was served at Yuan, Ming and Qing courts. Eaten wrapped in thin lotus-leaf pancakes with scallion strips and sweet bean sauce.

Where: Quanjude (founded 1864) runs the hung-oven school; time-honored duck restaurants cluster around Qianmen and Wangfujing.

Zhajiang noodles (炸酱面)

Boiled hand-pulled noodles mixed with a fried sauce of minced pork and yellow soybean paste, named for the way sauce bubbles in oil during cooking. Ranked among China's ten famous noodle dishes. Served with a plate of raw vegetable 'codings' — cucumber shreds, bean sprouts, radish strips and green soybeans — stirred in at the table.

Where: Common in old-Dongcheng and Xicheng noodle shops; most courtyard restaurants around Gulou serve a version at lunch.

Douzhir fermented mung-bean drink (豆汁儿)

A gray-green drink made from the fermented liquid left over when mung beans are processed for starch and glass noodles. Sour with a faint sweet finish and a strong signature odor from fermentation. Traditionally taken warm with crispy jiaoquan fried rings, pickled shredded vegetables or horseshoe-shaped sesame flatbread, usually as breakfast.

Where: A handful of old shops in Qianmen's Xianyukou area and near Niujie still brew it daily; expect queues of locals at opening.

Luzhu stewed offal with火烧 (卤煮火烧)

A southern-city dish from Nan Heng Street: pork intestines, lungs, belly and fried tofu simmered in a master broth, with unleavened huoshao bread added near mealtime so it soaks through. Descended from the Qing-court dish 'Su-zao pork', cheapened with offal for ordinary eaters. The bread is cut into a hash pattern, ingredients chopped on the board, broth ladled over.

Where: Founded shops such as the century-old Menku Hutang Luzhu operate near Qianmen; the dish is widespread in Xicheng old-town districts.

Chaogan stewed pork liver (炒肝)

A thickened soup of pork liver slices and large intestine bound with starch, heavy on garlic. Created around 1900 by the Huixianju restaurant in Qianmen's Xianyukou lane, developed from an older 'white broth offal' dish; after 1956 Huixianju merged with Tianxingju. Traditionally you sip around the bowl rim without a spoon, with small pork buns alongside.

Where: Yaoji Chaogan at Gulou is the best-known current exponent; Xianyukou food street near Qianmen has several stalls.

Copper-pot lamb hot pot (涮羊肉)

Beijing's signature winter hot pot: thin-sliced lamb swished seconds in a boiling copper chimney pot, dipped in sesame sauce with scallion, coriander and chili oil. The tradition also includes cabbage, tofu and vermicelli as standard companions. Copper pots existed by the Three Kingdoms era and the dish took its modern Beijing form by the late Qing dynasty.

Where: Jubaoyuan, founded in the Niujie Muslim quarter, is a benchmark; Donglaishun (est. 1903) popularized machine-cut lamb slices.

Lüdagun soybean-flour rolls (驴打滚)

A Manchu-Han court snack of steamed glutinous-rice dough rolled around red-bean paste, then coated in roasted soybean flour — the coating resembles a donkey rolling in dust, hence the name. Listed as a Qing-dynasty palace food; now a standard Beijing and north-China sweet. Cut into segments about a finger long, two or three per serving.

Where: Sold by weight at snack counters in Huguosi and Niujie areas, and at time-honored pastry shops like Daoxiangcun branches citywide.

Where to shop

Wangfujing Street (王府井大街)

Beijing's flagship commercial avenue, 1.8 kilometers long, with the southern section pedestrianized. The street dates to Yuan-dynasty 'Dingzi Street' and took its present name from the ten princely mansions built here in 1417. Anchor malls, the Foreign Languages Bookstore and a snack lane sit along it; most useful between 10:00 and 21:30.

Where: Dongcheng District, running north from East Chang'an Avenue to the Art Museum.

Dashilar (大栅栏)

A 275-meter commercial street west of Qianmen, part of a block defined by Langfang Toutiao, Zhushish and Meishi streets, holding 36 shops across 11 trades. Daily foot traffic runs 150,000–160,000, exceeding 200,000 on holidays. Traditional Chinese-medicine, silk and cloth stores founded in the Qing era operate here.

Where: Xicheng District, south of Tiananmen Square, immediately west of Qianmen Street.

Panjiayuan Flea Market (潘家园旧货市场)

China's largest secondhand market, with over 4,000 stalls run by dealers from 28 provinces and a dozen ethnic groups. Goods range from antique furniture, calligraphy scrolls and old books to jade, coins, shadow puppets and Mao-era memorabilia. Permanent shops open daily; weekend ground stalls are the liveliest time to visit.

Where: Southeast corner of the East Third Ring Road, Chaoyang District, beside Panjiayuan subway station.

Liulichang Culture Street (琉璃厂)

A 400-meter-long, 8-meter-wide street of antique books, paintings and the 'four treasures of the study' — brushes, ink, paper and inkstones. The name comes from the glazed-tile kilns opened here under the Yuan dynasty; the street was split into east and west halves when Nan Xinhua Street was cut through in 1926. Shops keep daytime hours, roughly 09:00–18:00.

Where: Southern Xicheng District, just off the Hepingmen subway stop.

Eating and shopping in Beijing: practical notes

Two food streets anchor the old city: Guijie (Dongzhimennei Street) stretches 1,400 meters with crayfish restaurants trading deep into the night, while Xianyukou near Qianmen concentrates breakfast snacks such as chaogan and douzhir. Duck restaurants cluster around Qianmen and Wangfujing; book a table for weekend dinners, since Quanjude's flagship outlets routinely queue past an hour in peak season.

For shopping, pair one modern street with one traditional one: Wangfujing's 1.8-kilometer avenue for department stores, then Dashilar's 275 meters for Qing-era medicine and cloth shops. Panjiayuan's 4,000-plus stalls are best on weekend mornings when ground sellers turn up, and bargaining there is expected — start at roughly half the quoted figure for collectibles.

Frequently Asked Questions

What food is Beijing most famous for?

Peking roast duck, documented as a court dish since the Ming court moved north in the 15th century and now split into hung-oven and closed-oven schools. Copper-pot lamb hot pot, zhajiang noodles and offal dishes like luzhu and chaogan form the street-food counterweight, most dating to the late Qing period.

Which food street should I choose, Guijie or Xianyukou?

Guijie is a 1,400-meter avenue geared to dinner and late-night dining, with crayfish as its signature order. Xianyukou, a lane off Qianmen, is the daytime choice for breakfast snacks such as chaogan, douzhir and stuffed pastries. Both sit within two subway stops of Tiananmen Square.

Where can I buy antiques or souvenirs in Beijing?

Panjiayuan Flea Market holds more than 4,000 stalls from 28 provinces, open daily with weekend ground stalls adding volume. Liulichang, 400 meters long near Hepingmen, covers calligraphy supplies, old books and inkstones. Dashilar's 36 traditional shops suit cloth, medicine and silk goods.

Is douzhir worth trying?

It is an acquired taste: a fermented mung-bean liquid with a sour flavor and a strong odor, drunk warm with fried jiaoquan rings. Beijingers treat it as an identity marker of old-city culture. Try a small bowl at an established shop in the Xianyukou or Niujie area before committing.

When is the best time to visit Wangfujing?

The 1.8-kilometer street, whose pedestrian southern section opens around 10:00, stays busy until stores close near 21:30. Weekday mornings are the quietest for the bookstores and mall flagships; the snack lane behind it runs later into the evening for street food.
