Overview: Tea Culture in Beijing

Beijing is a tea-consuming capital rather than a tea-growing one: the hills that surround the city carry no commercial tea gardens, so the teas poured in its teahouses are imported, most of them from the green-tea country south of the Yangtze. What the city contributed instead was a drinking culture. The jasmine-scented blend that became the household tea of the capital was scented by the layered method developed in Fuzhou, some 1,600 km to the south, and then packed and retailed in the old lanes around Qianmen and Dashilar by houses such as Wu Yutai, founded in 1887. The teahouse itself became the city's signature institution: Lao She's Teahouse, opened in 1988 in the Qianmen quarter, presents tea in a courtyard with performing arts, and the old guild-hall teahouses keep the older format of a covered bowl, a bamboo seat and a pot that is refilled for hours.

The tea in Beijing

Jasmine tea (茉莉花茶)

Jasmine tea is scented rather than fermented: green tea is layered with fresh jasmine blossoms over several nights in Fuzhou, so the scent is absorbed rather than added. The flowers are picked in the evening, when they are at their strongest. The capital's standard blend carries three scenting rounds and the better grades nine; the Fuzhou workshops that performed the scenting sit about 1,600 km from Beijing, so the capital's trade in this tea is a trade in a finished, imported product.

How to have it here: Jasmine is the default tea of the Beijing teahouse, served as a long-pour house brew and, in the old shops, weighed out into the covered bowl or the large glass cup of street tea. The standard serving uses about 5 g of leaf in a 150 ml bowl, water at 85-90°C and a first infusion of roughly 45 seconds, with 2 or 3 refills after it. Shops sell by the scenting count printed on the tin, from 3 rounds upward, and the 2 lowest grades carry the bulk of the trade.

Tieguanyin (铁观音)

Tieguanyin is a semi-fermented oolong from Anxi County in southern Fujian. The leaves are rolled into dark, tightly packed pellets, and the same pot can be brewed eight or ten times, with each round sweeter than the last. Anxi lies roughly 1,500 km from Beijing in the province's southern tea country, but the tea is a standard item on the counters of the Qianmen and Dashilar tea shops and on the tea lists of the capital's teahouses.

How to have it here: It is served in the small-pot way rather than the large-bowl way: a clay pot holding about 110 ml, charged with roughly 8 g of leaf, and pours of 15 ml or less taken in a run of 8 to 10 rounds. The pellets are dropped whole and never crushed. Because one pot covers a whole table rather than one cup, it suits the teahouse format of an afternoon seat bought by the hour.

Brick tea (砖茶)

Brick tea is compressed into blocks for long-distance transport and is the traditional tea of northern China and Inner Mongolia. Its trade runs on the caravan roads north from the tea districts toward the Mongolian border, and Beijing stood on the southern end of those routes, about 150 km from the border market at Zhangjiakou, receiving compressed brick in exchange for grain and cloth. The blocks are broken by hand into pieces, and a piece is boiled rather than steeped.

How to have it here: In the northern way the tea is cooked: a piece of about 5 grams in 300 ml of water brought to the boil and held there, then strained into a bowl and taken with milk. Brick is the tea of the hutong household and of the older guild-hall teahouses, and it is drunk for its strength rather than for any scent, unlike the jasmine blend that dominates the capital's shop counters.

Teahouses in Beijing

Lao She Teahouse (老舍茶馆)

Lao She's Teahouse in Beijing is the best-known traditional teahouse in the capital, with live performing arts in a courtyard setting and tea served all day. The house opened in 1988 in the Qianmen quarter, close to the Dashilar lanes where the old tea shops still stand. The layout follows the period teahouse: bamboo seating in a courtyard, a stage at one end, and the performance of traditional items such as tea-whisk or tea-ceremony demonstrations alongside opera and folk music. Tea is the ticket of entry for the audience, and a seat is charged by the cup rather than by the show.

Area: Qianmen area, Beijing

Wu Yutai (吴裕泰)

Wu Yutai is an old-brand tea house of the capital, founded in 1887 and long associated with the jasmine-scented green tea that became the city's household blend. Its trade is retail rather than performance: bricks and tins of scented tea, graded by the number of scenting rounds, sold over a counter in the old shopping streets. The founding year, 1887, places the business in the late Qing period, when the wholesale tea trade of the Qianmen market was at its height and the branded tea shop first became a recognisable form in the capital.

Area: Beijing

Huguang Guild Hall teahouse (湖广会馆茶馆)

The teahouse inside the Huguang Guild Hall keeps one of the capital's older room formats, in a building erected by Hubei and Hunan merchants during the Qing dynasty (1644-1911) as their provincial assembly hall. The guild-hall teahouse served travelling merchants and clerks who lodged in the capital for the trading season, and the seating is built for a long stay rather than a quick cup: a small table, a covered bowl, and tea refilled on a fixed round. Provincial guild halls of this kind numbered several in the city, and the teahouse was the everyday institution inside them.

Area: Beijing

Tea etiquette in Beijing

The capital's own gesture is the covered bowl, or gaiwan, and its discipline is the lid. A guest who has had enough lifts the lid a fraction and sets it back on the bowl; a guest who wants the pot refilled tilts the lid toward the server; and a guest who wants the service to stop turns the lid the other way. Water is poured at 85-90°C and the first steep left about 45 seconds before the second and third refills, and the bowl is never held while the table is talking, because the raised rim is read as a signal rather than a comfort.

Ordering in a teahouse follows the house, not the guest's taste. Tea is brought to the table and the price is set by the pot or by the seat, with a floor charge stated once at the beginning and no individual bills to divide later. In the old shops the same rule applies to the purchase: the shopkeeper brews 1 grade for the counter and a 2nd for comparison, and the discussion of the 3 to 9 scenting rounds and the harvest year precedes any money changing hands. Tasting before paying is ordinary commercial practice in the Qianmen tea trade, not a courtesy extended to strangers.

The guild-hall and teahouse format is built around a long seat. A pot ordered at 11 in the morning may stand on the table until mid-afternoon, and the server refills on a circuit rather than on request, so there is no need to call for the pot at all. Cups are drained in one go rather than sipped slowly, the spout is kept clear of the rim so that no drip lands on the saucer, and the pouring person keeps pouring for everyone present, in order of age, until the pot is empty. Spent leaf is tipped out rather than fished from with a spoon.

Frequently Asked Questions: Booking in Beijing

Which tea is the everyday tea of Beijing?

Jasmine-scented green tea. The scenting is done in Fuzhou, about 1,600 km south, and the finished blend is shipped to the capital, where the standard grade carries 3 scenting rounds and the better grades 9. It is brewed at 85-90°C with about 5 grams of leaf in a 150 ml bowl and refilled 2 or 3 times.

Why is Beijing's tea culture a city culture rather than a growing culture?

The hills around the city carry no commercial tea gardens, so every tea served in the capital is imported, most of it from the green-tea country south of the Yangtze. What the city built was the drinking and retail side of the trade: branded tea shops such as Wu Yutai, founded in 1887, and the teahouse format of a paid seat with a pot that is refilled for hours.

How do I signal that I have had enough tea in a Beijing teahouse?

With the lid of the covered bowl. Lifting it a fraction and resting it on the bowl says the tea is finished, while tilting it toward the server asks for a refill; turning it the other way ends the service. The same rules apply with a 150 ml gaiwan anywhere in the city, whether the house is a courtyard teahouse or a guild-hall tea room.

What is Lao She Teahouse and when did it open?

It is the best-known traditional teahouse in the capital, opened in 1988 in the Qianmen area, with live performing arts in a courtyard setting and tea served all day. The house works on the period model: bamboo seating, a stage, tea-ceremony and tea-whisk demonstrations alongside opera and folk music, and the price of a seat counted in cups of tea rather than in tickets to a show.

How is oolong served in a Beijing teahouse, and how many rounds does it give?

Small-pot style: about 8 grams of leaf in a 110 ml clay pot, with pours of 15 ml or less. The same pot runs 8 or 10 rounds, each sweeter than the last, because Tieguanyin holds its strength across many short infusions. The pellets are dropped whole, and one pot serves a whole table rather than a single cup, which is why a seat in a teahouse is priced for an afternoon.

What is brick tea and why is it drunk in the north?

It is tea compressed into blocks for long-distance transport, and it is the traditional tea of northern China and Inner Mongolia. The trade ran north on the caravan roads, with Beijing at the southern end of the route. A piece of roughly 5 grams is boiled in 300 ml of water, strained and taken with milk. Unlike the jasmine blend that dominates the capital's counters, brick is bought for strength, not scent.
