Overview: Tea Culture in Xiamen

Xiamen drinks the gongfu way: a small pot, small cups and short pours repeated many times. The leaf behind the habit is Tieguanyin, an oolong grown in Anxi County about 60 km northwest of the city; one portion of leaf serves eight to ten rounds, each sweeter than the last. The method itself is centuries old, was written down as early as the Qing, and in June 2008 the Chaozhou tea art it belongs to was inscribed on China's national intangible cultural heritage list. Tea tables stand in shops, offices and living rooms across the island as standard furniture.

The tea

Tieguanyin (铁观音)

Tieguanyin is a semi-fermented oolong from Anxi County in southern Fujian. The leaves are rolled into dark, tightly packed pellets, and the same pot can be brewed eight or ten times, with each round sweeter than the last. The cultivar was found at Xiping in Anxi during the Yongzheng reign and named, by one account, by the Qianlong emperor; traditional processing reaches 40-50 percent fermentation, and a well-made pot is said to hold fragrance through seven infusions.

How to have it here: Brewed gongfu-style across Fujian, Guangdong and Taiwan: a clay pot, leaf filling one half to two thirds of its capacity, boiling water, and the first infusions discarded. Quality grades follow fermentation degree, season and site, with autumn picking prized above spring and leaf standards of two or three leaves with a bud. In 2014 the Anxi Tieguanyin tea culture system entered China's second batch of important agricultural cultural heritage, and in 2022 it became a UNESCO Globally Important Agricultural Heritage System.

Gongfu tea (工夫茶)

Gongfu cha is a method rather than a tea: a small pot, a small cup, and short pours repeated many times. It is the standard way tea is served in Fujian and Guangdong, and it is why a good pu-erh cake can run to a dozen rounds. The name was first recorded in the Qing writer Yu Jiao's Cha Jia Feng Yue Ji, and the custom is more than 1,000 years old, carried south after the migrations of the Jin and Tang periods and now also practised in Hong Kong, Taiwan, Singapore and Thailand.

How to have it here: The full table - pot, strainer, pitcher and three to six cups - is ordinary equipment in Xiamen homes and shops, not a ceremony prop. Brewing follows a fixed sequence: warm the pot and cups, load the leaf, wet the leaf, pour, then clean. The maker pours in a counterclockwise circle known as Guan Gong touring the city, then taps the last drops evenly among the cups, known as Han Xin counting his troops. Cups are filled to eight tenths, following the saying that a full cup of tea deceives the guest while a full cup of wine honours him.

Oolong tea (乌龙茶)

Oolong is the category that holds Tieguanyin, and it is one of China's six tea types: the leaf is partly fermented, green in the centre and red at the edge. The method was worked out by Anxi tea makers around 1725, recorded in the county gazetteer as the third year of Yongzheng. A Fuzhou oolong warehouse existed by 1862, and Taiwanese oolong began export in 1866. Anxi, a county of 3,057 square kilometres with 460 villages, was named home of Chinese oolong in 1995.

How to have it here: In Xiamen oolong is not a separate order but the default leaf behind the table, and it is served in the same small pots with the same repeated pours. The main producing belt runs through Minnan and the Wuyi mountains in northern Fujian, with Chaoshan in Guangdong and Nantou, Chiayi and Taichung in Taiwan; Sichuan and Hunan now make smaller volumes. Choosing between a lightly oxidised style and a heavily roasted one is the first question a tea shop in Xiamen will put to you.

Da Hong Pao (大红袍)

Da Hong Pao is a Wuyi oolong from the cliffs of the Wuyi mountains, one of the teas traditionally reserved for the emperor. The mother trees stand in the Jiulongke rock face, where water runs down from above and reflected light shields the bushes from midday sun; a 1927 stone inscription by a Tianxin Temple monk still marks the site. Six mother bushes survive today, insured by the Wuyishan government for 100 million yuan. Even in the best years their yield is a few hundred grams, and since 2006 the bushes have been left unharvested.

How to have it here: Sold as yan cha in Xiamen tea shops and served in the same small pots as any other Wuyi rock tea, with a shorter, roast-heavy character than the Anxi leaf. Cultivated cuttings of the mother trees have been available since 1982, and by 1988 they were judged to match the flavour of the originals, which put rock tea of this type within reach of ordinary tables. It is a northern Fujian product rather than a Xiamen one, and is drunk here as a comparison pour against Tieguanyin.

Gongfu table manners in Xiamen

The host handles the pot and never drinks first: guests' cups are filled in one circling pour, the traditional move that keeps every cup equal, before the host takes any. When someone tops you up, tap your index and middle fingers twice on the table. Cups hold 20-30 ml; taking one in a single swallow is expected, not greedy. In Chaoshan, the country of the custom, the invitation to drink is spoken as shi, and the guest answers with the same word.

The first infusion goes down the drain as a rinse, and the wiki-recorded brewing sequence names this as a distinct step before the general brewing pass. Pours after that run 10-30 seconds, and one portion of leaf serves eight to ten rounds. Asking which round you are on is normal table talk, and a session typically runs from about 20 minutes to an hour, with the count of rounds announced as it climbs.

Water matters as much as leaf: natural hill spring water is ranked first, well and river water next, tap water last, and both the quality of the water and its temperature affect colour, aroma, taste and aftertaste. Leaf is stored in separate sealed tins for each variety, since a pot is never expected to carry two teas at once. To leave a session early, finish your cup and set it inverted on the tray, the table's understood signal for enough.

Frequently Asked Questions

What exactly is gongfu tea?

A brewing method, not a tea type: a small pot packed with leaf, quick pours of 10-30 seconds, and many rounds - eight to ten from a good Tieguanyin, up to a dozen from dense pu-erh. It is the everyday serving style across Fujian and Guangdong, which is why Xiamen tea tables carry the full small-pot kit. The custom has run for more than 1,000 years.

Is Tieguanyin grown in Xiamen?

No - it comes from Anxi County about 60 km northwest of the city, in the hills of southern Fujian. The cultivar was found at Xiping in Anxi during the Yongzheng reign, and the county is the documented origin of the leaf. Xiamen is the trading and drinking hub for it rather than the grower, and the Anxi tea villages are a day trip up the expressway.

How long does a gongfu session last?

Between about 20 minutes and an hour, depending on the leaf and the company. Count the first rinse and eight to ten drinkable rounds at 10-30 seconds each, with the pours stretching longer as the leaf gives out. Offices in Xiamen keep the pot refilling through the workday on the same portion of leaf.

When is Tieguanyin picked?

Twice a year, with autumn the prized picking and spring the second, followed by summer and mid-summer cuttings of lower grade. Leaf is taken as two or three leaves once the shoot has formed a bud, and pickers work to five rules: do not break the leaf, do not fold the leaf, do not crush the tip, do not take single leaves, and do not carry old stems.

How is Tieguanyin actually made?

The traditional Anxi process runs through a long list of stages, among them withering, sunning, resting, the shake-and-rest phase, pan firing, rolling, a first bake, a second bake, cloth-wrapped rolling, slow charcoal roasting and sorting. Traditional fermentation reaches 40-50 percent; lighter modern versions are sold as green Tieguanyin and heavier ones as red Tieguanyin, and grades follow fermentation, season and geology.

Is there anything official about the tea culture here?

Two listings apply. In June 2008 the Chaozhou gongfu tea art was inscribed on China's national intangible cultural heritage list, and in 2014 the Anxi Tieguanyin tea culture system entered the second batch of important agricultural cultural heritage systems; in 2022 it was recognised by the UN Food and Agriculture Organisation as a Globally Important Agricultural Heritage System.
