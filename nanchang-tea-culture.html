Overview: Tea Culture in Nanchang

Nanchang sits on the Gan River plain with little high ground of its own, so its two named teas grow elsewhere in Jiangxi: Lushan Yunwu from gardens between 800 and 1,100 metres on Mount Lu, about 120 km north in Jiujiang, and Ninghong, a black tea from the hills around Xiushui - the old Ningzhou - roughly 240 km to the west.

The tea

Lushan Yunwu (庐山云雾)

Lushan Yunwu is a green tea grown on Mount Lu in Jiujiang, Jiangxi, where the gardens sit between about 800 and 1,100 metres and mist shrouds the mountain roughly 190 days a year - the 'cloud and mist' of the name. Recorded as tribute tea since the Song dynasty (10th-13th century), it is plucked as a bud with one leaf and fired into twisted, downy strands.

How to have it here: In Nanchang it is stocked by tea shops citywide and poured in ordinary restaurants; the classic trip is to Mount Lu itself, about 120 km north or some 40 minutes by high-speed rail, where spring lots reach the stalls by mid-April. Labels worth reading on the pack are the pre-Qingming and pre-Grain-Rain picking dates.

Ninghong (宁红茶)

Ninghong is a black tea from the hills of northern Jiangxi; the name comes from Ningzhou, the old prefecture now called Xiushui, where the gardens climb the slopes above the river valley. The leaf is rolled into tight, dark strands with a honeyed aroma. An export tea since the 19th century, its making craft now holds a national intangible-heritage listing.

How to have it here: Brew it at 90-95°C for two to three minutes; in Nanchang it appears mostly at tea shops and in gift boxes rather than on restaurant tables. Xiushui, the growing county, lies roughly 240 km west of the city, about three hours by expressway.

Tea manners in Nanchang

Jiangxi tables keep the old rules: a cup is filled to seven-tenths - 'a full cup cheats the guest', as the local saying puts it - and the eldest guest is poured first, with the pot handle turned away from them. Two-finger tapping thanks the pourer. Declining a first pour outright is blunt; taking at least a little is the politer course.

At a Nanchang tea shop the host will rinse the leaf and pour the first round away for green and black tea alike before the drinkable round follows. Asking after the origin - Mount Lu or Xiushui - and the picking year is expected conversation, because both teas are graded by spring dates and the price gap between spring and summer lots is wide.

Frequently Asked Questions

What kind of tea is Lushan Yunwu?

A green tea from Mount Lu in Jiujiang, grown between roughly 800 and 1,100 metres where mist covers the mountain about 190 days a year. The high, slow growth keeps the leaf tender, and the tea has been recorded as tribute since the Song dynasty, ten centuries back. The strand is twisted and downy, plucked as one bud with one leaf.

Is Lushan Yunwu grown in Nanchang?

No - the gardens are on Mount Lu near Jiujiang, about 120 km to the north, reachable in roughly 40 minutes by high-speed rail from Nanchang. Nanchang is the provincial capital where the tea is traded and drunk, not the producing ground. Day trips to the mountain combine the gardens with the summer resort town on the summit.

What is Ninghong tea?

A black tea from Xiushui county in northern Jiangxi - named for Ningzhou, the prefecture that county once was - rolled into tight dark strands with a honeyed aroma. It shipped as an export tea through the 19th century, and its making craft is listed as national intangible heritage. The county is roughly 240 km west of Nanchang.

Which makes a better gift from Nanchang?

Lushan Yunwu for green-tea drinkers, Ninghong for black - both carry Jiangxi origin marks and spring picking dates that set the grade. A pre-Qingming Yunwu lot from the April window costs clearly more than later picking, while Ninghong gift boxes usually state the strand grade on the lid. Tea shops in the city centre stock both side by side.
